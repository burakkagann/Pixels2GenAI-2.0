## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

A Penrose tiling is a covering of the plane by **two tile shapes** such that the resulting pattern has **no translational symmetry** — it never repeats, anywhere, at any distance. Roger Penrose discovered the simplest such tilings in 1974 [1]; physicist Dan Shechtman found the same five-fold-symmetric pattern in real metal alloys in 1982, kicking off the field of **quasicrystals** and earning him the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [2]. The connection is exact: shoot X-rays at a quasicrystal and the diffraction pattern shows ten-fold rotational peaks but no translational order — the same statistical signature a Penrose tiling produces.

For us, the Penrose tiling is the third dialect of the same L-system idea you have seen in 4.3.1 (organic plants) and 4.3.2 (geometric snowflakes). Here the "rule" doesn't rewrite a string — it **deflates** a triangle into smaller triangles using the **golden ratio** as the scaling factor. Apply the deflation rule to a starting "wheel" of ten triangles, repeat, and out comes the famous P3 (rhombus) tiling with its star, sun, ace, deuce, and jack configurations. The non-repeating, almost-periodic look is the inevitable consequence of using $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5) / 2$ as the scale factor — an irrational number whose continued-fraction expansion has the slowest possible convergence to any rational, which is why the tiling can never close on itself.

## Quick start — five deflations <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
import math, cmath
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw

GOLDEN = (1 + 5 ** 0.5) / 2

def subdivide(triangles):
    out = []
    for color, a, b, c in triangles:
        if color == 0:   # thin Robinson triangle
            p = a + (b - a) / GOLDEN
            out.append((0, c, p, b))
            out.append((1, p, c, a))
        else:            # thick Robinson triangle
            q = b + (a - b) / GOLDEN
            r = b + (c - b) / GOLDEN
            out.append((1, r, c, a))
            out.append((1, q, r, b))
            out.append((0, r, q, a))
    return out

# Decagonal wheel — 10 thin triangles meeting at the centre
triangles = []
for i in range(10):
    b = cmath.exp((2*i + 1) * math.pi * 1j / 10)
    c = cmath.exp((2*i - 1) * math.pi * 1j / 10)
    if i % 2 == 0: b, c = c, b
    triangles.append((0, 0+0j, b, c))

for _ in range(5):
    triangles = subdivide(triangles)

img = Image.new("RGB", (800, 800), (18, 20, 30))
draw = ImageDraw.Draw(img)
scale = 360
cx, cy = 400, 400
for color, a, b, c in triangles:
    pts = [(cx + scale * z.real, cy + scale * z.imag) for z in (a, b, c)]
    fill = (210, 215, 90) if color == 0 else (255, 175, 90)
    draw.polygon(pts, fill=fill, outline=(40, 40, 55))
img.save("simple_penrose.png")
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Aperiodic tilings and the golden ratio

A periodic tiling has a **translation vector** $t$ such that shifting the entire pattern by $t$ produces the identical tiling. Squares, triangles, hexagons all tile periodically. An **aperiodic** tiling has no such vector — no shift ever maps the pattern onto itself. For the longest time it was unclear whether such tilings even existed with a finite tile set; in 1966 Robert Berger constructed one with 20,426 tiles, and in 1974 Penrose found the now-famous P3 tiling with just **two**: a fat rhombus and a thin rhombus, joined according to local matching rules [1].

The Robinson triangle representation cuts each rhombus in half along its short diagonal, yielding two isoceles triangles. The thin rhombus splits into two **thin** Robinson triangles (vertex angles 72°/72°/36°); the thick rhombus splits into two **thick** Robinson triangles (vertex angles 108°/36°/36°). Both pairs of triangles share their golden-ratio side-length relationship: the ratio of long side to short side is exactly $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5) / 2$ in both [3].

That irrationality is what guarantees aperiodicity. If $\varphi$ were rational, the tiling could close after some finite count; because it isn't, it can't.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know · quasicrystals predate Penrose">
Dan Shechtman saw five-fold-symmetric diffraction patterns from aluminium-manganese alloys in 1982. The crystallographic community refused to publish him for two years — five-fold symmetry was supposed to be impossible in a real crystal. When the paper finally appeared in 1984, the connection to Penrose tilings was immediate: the diffraction pattern of a quasi-periodic atomic arrangement gives exactly the Bragg peaks predicted by the Penrose tile vertex statistics [2]. Linus Pauling famously dismissed the discovery for years; Shechtman won the Nobel anyway in 2011.
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — The deflation rule

The simplest way to generate a Penrose tiling is **deflation**: repeatedly subdivide each existing tile into smaller tiles according to fixed rules. The Robinson triangles deflate as follows:

- A **thin** Robinson triangle (`color = 0`, vertices $a, b, c$ where $a$ is the apex) subdivides into one thin + one thick:
  - Compute $p = a + (b - a) / \varphi$ — the point that splits edge $ab$ at the inverse-golden ratio.
  - Output triangles `(0, c, p, b)` (thin) and `(1, p, c, a)` (thick).
- A **thick** Robinson triangle subdivides into one thin + two thicks:
  - Compute $q = b + (a - b) / \varphi$ and $r = b + (c - b) / \varphi$.
  - Output `(1, r, c, a)`, `(1, q, r, b)`, `(0, r, q, a)`.

The factor `1 / φ ≈ 0.618` is what makes each child triangle smaller by exactly the right amount to tile its parent perfectly. After $n$ deflations, the original tile count $N_0$ grows to roughly $N_0$ times $\varphi$ raised to the power $2n$ — close to a 2.6× multiplication per step.

This is structurally the same idea as the Koch snowflake's rule (4.3.2) — replace each shape with a small set of children — but the children share their parent's shape only **up to type** (thin/thick), not up to similarity. That is what makes Penrose tilings aperiodic where Koch curves are merely self-similar.

### Concept 3 — Aperiodicity in practice

Three properties of Penrose tilings are worth singling out:

- **No translational symmetry.** No shift maps the tiling onto itself. Provably so — the proof uses the irrationality of $\varphi$ [3].
- **Five-fold rotational symmetry.** The decagonal wheel of 10 starting triangles gives the whole tiling a 5-fold rotational symmetry around its centre. The local order is high — there are vertex configurations called "star," "sun," "ace," "deuce," "queen," "king," "jack" — but globally, the tiling never repeats.
- **The golden ratio is everywhere.** Tile counts, edge lengths, angle ratios, vertex configurations — all involve $\varphi$ at some level. This is the cleanest mathematical example of $\varphi$ appearing in geometry; the Fibonacci-spiral cliché has nothing on it [4].

<Admonition type="important">
Penrose tilings are not just curiosities — they are the cleanest, most beautiful example of **emergent structure from local rules**. The same idea drives cellular automata (1.2.2), L-systems (4.3.1), and ultimately the way trained neural networks (Module 9) produce coherent global behaviour from local connections. Aperiodicity is the rigorous version of "looking organised without being predictable."
</Admonition>

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Scaling check** — $\varphi$ raised to the tenth power is about 122.99. So $10 \cdot \varphi^{10}$ is about 1,230, versus the actual 890 — close to the right order of magnitude, with the discrepancy explained by the fact that thin and thick triangles deflate into different child counts (2 vs 3), so the average growth factor is not exactly $\varphi^2$. The exact recurrence for the count is "next tile count equals two times current thin + three times current thick" where the thin and thick subcounts evolve in lockstep.

**Sun configurations** — you can find 4–6 visible suns in the depth-5 tiling. The exact count depends on which exact tile boundary you take; on the colored_penrose rendering with sharp orange tiles, the configurations stand out clearly.

**5-fold vs 10-fold** — the underlying wheel has 10 starting triangles, but they alternate orientation (every other one is flipped). Rotating by $36°$ swaps the "left" and "right" triangles, which has a different colour, so the colouring breaks the 10-fold symmetry down to 5-fold. The pattern of edges by itself does have 10-fold symmetry.

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Convert to rhombi.** Merge adjacent thin-thin and thick-thick triangle pairs into single rhombus tiles. The result is the canonical P3 tiling drawing — visually cleaner than the triangle version because each rhombus is a single coloured polygon.
- **Penrose chickens.** The cult-classic "Penrose chickens" tiling replaces each rhombus with a curved-edge tile that looks like a chicken. Same combinatorics, different artwork. A chickens-style replacement preserves the aperiodicity because it only deforms each tile's boundary.
- **3D quasicrystal.** Generalise the deflation to icosahedral 3D using golden-ratio scaling and 6-fold symmetric starting tetrahedra. The result is the cleanest visualisation of a real quasicrystal lattice.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

A Penrose tiling is an aperiodic covering of the plane with two tile shapes. The Robinson triangle representation expresses both rhombi as triangles, and the **deflation rule** subdivides each triangle into 2 or 3 smaller children using $1/\varphi$ as the scale factor. Tile count grows as $\varphi^\{2n\}$ with deflation depth. The golden ratio's irrationality is what guarantees aperiodicity — no shift ever maps the tiling onto itself, even though the local geometry contains highly ordered configurations (Sun, Star, etc.). Five-fold rotational symmetry around the decagonal wheel centre coexists with the global non-repetition — the same combination later observed in real quasicrystals by Shechtman, vindicating Penrose's purely mathematical construction with physical evidence. Conceptually this completes Module 04: organic L-systems (4.3.1), geometric L-systems (4.3.2), and now aperiodic deflation L-systems — three dialects of the same replacement-rule paradigm.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- **Wrong subdivision points.** The deflation uses $p = a + (b - a) / \varphi$, not $p = a + (b - a) \cdot \varphi$. The factor `1 / φ ≈ 0.618` makes the children smaller; multiplying by `φ` would make them bigger and the tiling would explode.
- **Wrong wheel parity.** Every other triangle in the initial wheel needs its $b$ and $c$ vertices swapped, otherwise the wheel doesn't tile correctly and you get gaps or overlaps in the centre.
- **Mixing complex and float.** The implementation here uses Python `complex` numbers throughout — easier to express rotations and linear combinations. Mixing in `(x, y)` tuples halfway through is a source of bugs; pick one and stick with it.
- **Expecting periodic patches.** It is tempting to look for repeating sub-patches as the deflation depth increases. Don't — the whole point is that there are none. The tiling has strong **local** order but no **global** period.
