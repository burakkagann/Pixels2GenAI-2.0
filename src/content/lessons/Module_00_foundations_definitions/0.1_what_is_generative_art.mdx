## Big question <a id="big-question"></a>

In 1971 Sol LeWitt gave a wall drawing its form as a few sentences of instruction [1]:

> On a wall surface, any continuous stretch of wall, using a hard pencil, place fifty points at random. The points should be evenly distributed over the area of the wall. All of the points should be connected by straight lines.

Anyone can carry that out, and no two walls will match. A program can carry it out too, and the figure below shows three of its attempts.

<Figure

  caption="Three runs of one short program. It follows LeWitt's sentences, but these are coding output, not LeWitt works."

So who made these pictures: the person who wrote the rule, or the thing that carried it out? This lesson gives you the vocabulary to argue either side.

<ol class="obj-list">

## Part 1 — A system that runs on its own <a id="part-1"></a>

A widely quoted definition comes from the artist and theorist Philip Galanter [2]:

> Generative art refers to any art practice where the artist uses a system, such as a set of natural language rules, a computer program, a machine, or other procedural invention, which is set into motion with some degree of autonomy contributing to or resulting in a completed work of art.

Notice what is missing: the word "digital". LeWitt's sentences are natural language rules, so they count, and a program counts for the same reason. What matters, Galanter writes, is "the system to which the artist cedes partial or total subsequent control" [2]. Figure 2 shows that handover.

<Figure
  src="/lesson-media/Module_00_foundations_definitions/0.1_what_is_generative_art/making_versus_system.png"
  alt="Two flow diagrams. In the first, an artist makes every mark of one work. In the second, an artist writes rules for a system, and the system produces work 1, work 2 and work 3."
  caption="The artist's job moves one step back: from making each mark to designing what makes the marks."
  num={2}
  role="diagram"
/>

Galanter also refuses a hard border: "generative art is a fuzzy set" [2]. Boden and Edmonds go further and split computer-based work into categories such as evolutionary art and robot art [3]. For a first map, one question sorts most cases: who decides each mark?

| Case | Who decides each mark? | How generative? |
|---|---|---|
| A portrait painted by hand, stroke by stroke | The painter | Not generative |
| The same portrait painted in a drawing app | The painter; the software is a tool | Digital, not generative |
| A program whose every coordinate was typed in by hand | The author, in advance | Barely: nothing is left to the system |
| LeWitt's sentences, carried out by a team | The rule, plus the people following it | Clearly generative, no computer involved |
| A program that places fifty random points | The rule and its random numbers | Clearly generative |
| An image made from a text prompt | A trained model; the person picks words and results | Generative, with authorship in dispute [4] |

The last row is the one people argue about today, and the next lesson separates the terms that argument needs.

## Part 2 — Between order and disorder <a id="part-2"></a>

Once a system runs on its own, the artist's main choice is how much freedom to give it. Galanter maps that choice with the idea of effective complexity, borrowed from the physicist Murray Gell-Mann. Something "almost entirely random" scores near zero, and so does "something completely regular" [2]. Structure worth looking at lies in between.

<Figure
  src="/lesson-media/Module_00_foundations_definitions/0.1_what_is_generative_art/order_and_disorder_chart.png"
  alt="A bell-shaped curve of effective complexity, low at total order, high in between and low at total disorder. Below it, three grids of nested squares: perfectly regular, slightly wobbling, and a tangle of lines."
  caption="A sketch of the idea, not a measurement. The three panels come from one program with one number changed."
  num={3}
  role="diagram"
/>

Vera Molnár worked on this dial for decades. From the late 1950s she followed rules by hand, calling the method her "machine imaginaire". She reached a real computer in 1968 and became known for adding about one percent of disorder to an ordered structure [5, 6]. Georg Nees's plotter drawing *Schotter* (Gravel) shows the whole range at once: rows of squares that loosen from top to bottom [7]. Figure 4 is a study of that idea written for this lesson.

<Figure
  src="/lesson-media/Module_00_foundations_definitions/0.1_what_is_generative_art/rows_of_squares.png"
  alt="Rows of black outlined squares. The top rows are neat and aligned; further down the squares shift and rotate until they overlap freely."
  caption="One rule whose disorder grows with the row number. Written for this lesson after Nees's idea; it is not his work."
  num={4}
  role="diagram"
/>

Artists working now describe the same balance. Tyler Hobbs says that a totally predictable system would give him "perfect 'control'", but that this "usually leads to uninteresting results" [8].

## Part 3 — A short history <a id="part-3"></a>

The system has changed form many times: written instruction, plotter program, rule base, seeded script, trained model. The question of who decides has stayed the same.

| When | Who | The system |
|---|---|---|
| 70,000+ years ago | Blombos Cave, South Africa | Patterned engravings on ochre. Galanter offers these as very early rule-like making and admits the claim is contested [2, 9] |
| 1790s | A dice game printed under Mozart's name | Dice choose among prepared bars of music. The attribution to Mozart is doubtful [10] |
| 1965 | Georg Nees, A. Michael Noll, Frieder Nake | Among the first public shows of computer-generated images, in Stuttgart and New York [11] |
| 1967 | Sol LeWitt | The written instruction as artwork: "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art" [12] |
| 1973 | Harold Cohen, AARON | A drawing program built from rules and forms Cohen defined, developed for four decades [13] |
| 2001 | Casey Reas and Ben Fry, Processing | A programming language for visual artists that made code sketching widely available [14] |
| 2020–21 | Art Blocks; Tyler Hobbs, *Fidenza* | The artist's script generates each piece from a seed at the moment of minting [15] |
| 2022 | Refik Anadol, *Unsupervised* | A generative model trained on 138,151 images from MoMA's collection, moving continuously through its latent space [16] |

Compare the last entry with AARON. Cohen wrote AARON's rules himself, whereas the rules behind *Unsupervised* came out of training on data, so nobody typed them in. Figure 5 marks that break, and [lesson 0.2](/lessons/0.2) takes it apart.

## Summary

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

- Generative art is defined by a system that runs with some autonomy, not by computers. Digital and generative are separate questions.
- The label is a matter of degree. Ask who decides each mark.
- Much of the craft lies in setting the amount of disorder: total order and total randomness both say little.
- The system's form has changed from written rules to trained models, while the question of authorship has stayed open.

</Admonition>
