## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

Alan Turing's last published paper before his death — *The chemical basis of morphogenesis*, 1952 — proposed that animal coat patterns (zebra stripes, leopard spots, giraffe reticulation) arise from two chemicals diffusing through skin at different rates while reacting with each other [1]. The paper went unread for decades; experimental confirmation came only in the 1990s, with the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction and synthetic catalytic systems. The simplest model that demonstrates Turing's mechanism in code is **Gray-Scott** — two chemicals U and V, with U slowly converting into V via the autocatalytic reaction `U + 2V → 3V`, while U is fed in and V is removed. By tuning two parameters (feed rate and kill rate) you can reproduce spots, stripes, labyrinths, mitosis-like splittings, and travelling waves. The whole model is two lines of NumPy.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Recognise reaction-diffusion as the canonical mechanism for self-organised pattern formation in biology.</li>

</ol>

## Quick start — Gray-Scott in two lines <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
def step(U, V):
    Lu = laplacian(U); Lv = laplacian(V)
    uvv = U * V * V
    U = U + DT * (DU * Lu - uvv + FEED * (1 - U))
    V = V + DT * (DV * Lv + uvv - (FEED + KILL) * V)
    return np.clip(U, 0, 1), np.clip(V, 0, 1)
```
</CodeBlock>

<Admonition type="tip">
The pattern *stabilises* — once the spots have formed, they neither grow nor shrink. That is the defining property of Turing patterns: they are *stationary*, not transient. They are also *robust* to small perturbations, which is why animal coats stay striped or spotted across an animal's lifetime even though skin cells are constantly turning over.
</Admonition>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Why reaction + diffusion produces patterns

A single chemical that diffuses just smooths out — gradients flatten and the system reaches uniform equilibrium. Reaction-diffusion has two chemicals with **different diffusion rates** and a reaction that destabilises uniformity at intermediate spatial scales. Turing showed mathematically that this combination creates a *band* of unstable wavelengths; the system spontaneously oscillates at that scale until it locks into a stationary pattern [1].

Concretely, in Gray-Scott:

- **U** is fed in everywhere at rate `FEED * (1 - U)`. It diffuses fast (`DU = 0.16`).
- **V** is created from U via the autocatalytic step `U + 2V → 3V` (the `U * V * V` term). It diffuses half as fast (`DV = 0.08`).
- **V** is also removed at rate `(FEED + KILL) * V`.

Different diffusion rates mean V "leaks" away more slowly than U; the autocatalysis means V grows where it already exists. The combination generates self-reinforcing spots of V.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Turing's 1952 paper">
Turing's paper appeared posthumously — he died in 1954 — and was largely ignored by chemists for forty years because the predicted reactions hadn't been observed. Patrick De Kepper's 1990 experiments in a continuously fed reactor finally produced clean Turing patterns in synthetic chemistry [2]. Biologically, the mechanism is now confirmed in fish-skin patterning, zebra stripes, and digit formation in vertebrate limbs.
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Discrete Laplacian with periodic boundaries

The Laplacian `∇²f = ∂²f/∂x² + ∂²f/∂y²` measures how much a value differs from its local neighbourhood average. The simplest discrete form is the **5-point stencil**: `(N + S + E + W) - 4 * centre`.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="laplacian_demo.py">
```python
def laplacian(field):
    return (np.roll(field, +1, axis=0) + np.roll(field, -1, axis=0)
            + np.roll(field, +1, axis=1) + np.roll(field, -1, axis=1)
            - 4 * field)
```
</CodeBlock>

`np.roll` cyclically shifts the array — implementing **periodic boundary conditions** for free. Periodic boundaries are essential here because reaction-diffusion is exquisitely sensitive to boundary effects; a Dirichlet boundary (clamp the value at the edge) would distort the pattern near the edges.

<Admonition type="important">
The 9-point stencil (including diagonals) is slightly more isotropic but costs 50% more flops. For Gray-Scott the 5-point stencil is fine — the patterns are large compared to a pixel, so the small anisotropy does not show.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — The (FEED, KILL) parameter space

Gray-Scott has effectively two parameters: FEED and KILL. The combination determines the regime:

| FEED  | KILL  | Pattern                       |
| ----- | ----- | ----------------------------- |
| 0.020 | 0.051 | "Spots" — V dots in U background       |
| 0.040 | 0.060 | "Coral" — labyrinth + spot mix         |
| 0.055 | 0.062 | "Stripes" — V bands separated by U     |
| 0.014 | 0.045 | "Mitosis" — spots split mid-frame      |
| 0.062 | 0.061 | "Worm" — meandering V trails           |
| 0.090 | 0.057 | "Waves" — travelling fronts            |

Robert Munafo's interactive map of the (FEED, KILL) plane is the canonical reference. Tiny parameter changes produce qualitatively different patterns — exactly what Turing predicted.

<Admonition type="note">
"Mitosis" is the most famous regime — V spots literally split and reproduce, visually similar to cell division. This is the smoking-gun evidence that reaction-diffusion captures something morphogenetic, not just decorative.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- How long (in frames) does the pattern take to stabilise?
- Why are the spots not all the same size?
- What would happen if you set `DU == DV`?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Stabilisation time** — typically 30–50 frames at default settings. The first few iterations are dominated by the initial noise distribution; somewhere in the middle the autocatalysis takes over and spots start to coalesce; by frame ~40 the pattern is essentially fixed.

**Variable spot sizes** — partly initial-condition asymmetry, partly that the Gray-Scott regime allows a *range* of stable spot sizes rather than a single one. Watch the GIF closely and you can see medium spots occasionally "splitting" or "merging" before the system fully relaxes.

**Equal diffusion** — Turing's mathematical proof requires `DU > DV` for instability to occur. With equal diffusion, the system smooths to uniformity rather than forming patterns. This is the canonical test for whether your simulation is *really* a Turing system or just a chemistry simulation that happens to look patterned.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Cross parameter regimes">

**Goals**

1. **Spots regime**: `FEED = 0.020, KILL = 0.051`. Small, well-separated dots.
2. **Stripes regime**: `FEED = 0.055, KILL = 0.062`. Long parallel V bands.
3. **Mitosis regime**: `FEED = 0.014, KILL = 0.045`. Spots that split.
4. **Waves regime**: `FEED = 0.090, KILL = 0.057`. Travelling V fronts.

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — what to expect">

Small, discrete spots — like leopard rosettes. The pattern stabilises quickly. With FEED low and KILL relatively high, V cannot grow into large connected regions before being killed.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — what to expect">

Stripes — like a zebra or a fingerprint. With higher FEED and slightly higher KILL, V can grow long but cannot fill broad regions because the kill term dominates wide V regions.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — what to expect">

Spots that visibly split and reproduce. This is Turing's most spectacular regime — you can literally watch "cells" divide. The biological analogue is real and is currently being studied as a possible mechanism in zebrafish embryo patterning [3].

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 4 — what to expect">

Travelling waves of V across the grid. The system never settles into a stable spatial pattern; instead, fronts of V move continuously, sometimes annihilating each other on collision. Looks like the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction in a Petri dish.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

```python
def step(U, V):
    Lu = laplacian(U)
    Lv = laplacian(V)
    uvv = U * V * V
    U = U + DT * (DU * Lu - uvv + FEED * (1 - U))
    V = V + DT * (DV * Lv + uvv - (FEED + KILL) * V)
    np.clip(U, 0, 1, out=U)
    np.clip(V, 0, 1, out=V)
    return U, V
```

The `np.clip(..., out=field)` form modifies in place — slightly faster than creating a new array.

</Dropdown>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Reaction-diffusion is two chemicals plus three lines of physics. The Laplacian via `np.roll` is the discrete approximation to spatial diffusion; the multiplication `U * V * V` is the autocatalytic reaction; FEED and KILL parameterise the chemistry. With `DU > DV` and the right (FEED, KILL) combination, the system spontaneously breaks uniformity into spots, stripes, or labyrinths — Turing's 1952 prediction, now confirmed in animal coat patterns, vertebrate limb development, and synthetic chemistry. The whole model fits in 30 lines of Python, runs in real time on a modest grid, and produces visuals indistinguishable from biological reality.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Setting `DU == DV`. The system smooths to uniformity — no Turing instability.
- Using Dirichlet boundaries instead of periodic. Patterns distort near the edges.
- Picking (FEED, KILL) outside the active regime. You get a uniform field or unbounded V. Stay on the documented map.
- `DT = 2.0`. The explicit Euler integrator goes unstable; values explode within a few steps.
- Not clipping to [0, 1]. Numerical drift takes one field slightly negative and the autocatalysis amplifies it.
