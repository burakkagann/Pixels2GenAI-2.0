## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

A whirlpool is two forces at right angles. Each water particle is pulled inward toward the drain and pushed sideways along a circle — the inward pull alone would collapse the field to a point, the tangential push alone would just orbit forever. Their sum is a *spiral*. In this lesson you will reuse the particle-system loop from lesson 5.1.1 but replace the wind force with the vector sum of a radial attractor and a tangential rotator. The change is fifteen lines of code; the visual leap is enormous.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Compute radial and tangential unit vectors for any particle from its offset to a centre point.</li>
  <li>Recognise that a tangential vector is a 90° rotation of the radial — `(-y, x)` is the entire trick.</li>
  <li>Tune the balance of inward pull and tangential force to produce different vortex shapes — tight spiral, slow whirl, or unstable disc.</li>
  <li>Use frame-to-frame fading on the canvas to render motion trails without storing per-particle history.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — see the spiral <a id="quick-start"></a>

Each frame, every particle gets two forces. The radial force is `-INWARD_PULL * offset`. The tangential force is `TANGENTIAL_FORCE * unit_tangential`, where the unit tangential is the radial unit vector rotated by 90 degrees.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
offsets = positions - CENTRE
distances = np.linalg.norm(offsets, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-3
unit_radial = offsets / distances
unit_tangential = np.column_stack([-unit_radial[:, 1], unit_radial[:, 0]])

radial_force = -INWARD_PULL * offsets
tangential_force = TANGENTIAL_FORCE * unit_tangential / np.sqrt(distances)

velocities = (velocities + radial_force + tangential_force) * DRAG
positions += velocities
```
</CodeBlock>

<Admonition type="tip">
The reason the spiral closes inward — instead of orbiting forever — is the **drag** term. Without `velocities *= DRAG` (with `DRAG ≈ 0.985`) the energy injected by the tangential force accumulates and the particles fly off. Drag is the physical stand-in for water viscosity or air resistance.
</Admonition>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Radial vector = offset from centre

For any particle at position `p` and a fixed centre `C`, the **radial vector** is `p - C`. Its direction points outward from the centre to the particle; its magnitude is the distance. Dividing the radial vector by its own magnitude gives the **unit radial** — a length-1 vector pointing outward.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="radial_demo.py">
```python
offsets = positions - CENTRE                           # (N, 2) radial vectors
distances = np.linalg.norm(offsets, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-3
unit_radial = offsets / distances                      # (N, 2) outward unit vectors
```
</CodeBlock>

The `+ 1e-3` on distance is a numerical safety net — if a particle lands exactly on the centre, dividing by zero crashes. Adding a tiny epsilon lets the division proceed and the particle gets nudged outward on the next frame.

<Admonition type="note">
Radial vectors are the single most useful building block in centre-based forces. The same calculation drives gravitational attractors, magnetic poles, repulsive obstacles, and goal-seeking steering behaviours. Once you can compute `unit_radial`, all of those become one-line changes to the force.
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Tangential = 90° rotation of radial

A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. In 2D, a 90-degree counter-clockwise rotation takes any vector `(x, y)` to `(-y, x)` — no trigonometry, no matrix multiply.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="tangential_demo.py">
```python
unit_tangential = np.column_stack([-unit_radial[:, 1], unit_radial[:, 0]])
```
</CodeBlock>

That single line is what makes the vortex rotate. Multiply by a positive scalar to spin counter-clockwise, by a negative scalar to spin clockwise. Multiply by `1 / sqrt(distance)` and the rotation speeds up near the centre — which is why real whirlpools have visibly faster-spinning hearts.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Why √distance, not distance?">
The choice of falloff is aesthetic, not physical. With `1 / distance` (Newtonian) the inner particles whip into a streak. With `1 / sqrt(distance)` they spin fast but stay visible. With `distance` (constant angular velocity) the spiral looks rigid like a fan blade. Pick the falloff that matches the look you want — there is no canonical "right" answer.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — Trails for free with canvas fading

To show motion paths without storing per-particle history, multiply the entire canvas by a fade factor (0.82 in our case) before drawing the next frame's particles. Old pixels darken over time; new bright pixels are added on top. After ten frames a fresh particle pixel is at 18% brightness — the visual signature of a trail.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="fade_demo.py">
```python
canvas = (canvas.astype(np.float32) * TRAIL_FADE).astype(np.uint8)
canvas[ys, xs] = PARTICLE_COLOUR
```
</CodeBlock>

<Admonition type="important">
The same trick is how oscilloscopes drew persistent traces in the 1960s — phosphor on the screen would fade at a known rate, so the past was literally darker than the present. The technique is older than computers; the code is newer.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- Why do particles closer to the centre spin visibly faster?
- What would happen if `INWARD_PULL` were exactly zero?
- What does `DRAG = 0.985` actually do — and why is it less than 1?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Faster centre spin** — the tangential force scales as `1 / sqrt(distance)`. At distance 10 it is `≈ 0.32`; at distance 100 it is `≈ 0.1`. The inner particles get three times the tangential push per frame and circle the centre at proportionally higher angular velocity.

**`INWARD_PULL = 0`** — particles would orbit the centre at whatever radius they spawned at. With drag in the mix they would slowly spiral outward (drag eats radial momentum less than tangential, so the orbit decays unevenly). The classic disc-shape of a vortex needs both forces.

**Drag less than 1** — `velocities *= 0.985` removes 1.5% of speed per frame. It is not a force, it is a multiplicative damping that keeps the system stable. Without it the tangential force pumps energy in continuously and the particles eventually fly out faster than the canvas can show.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Reshape the vortex">

Edit the constants at the top of `vortex.py` and re-render after each change.

**Goals**

1. **Tight, dense vortex**: `INWARD_PULL=0.15, TANGENTIAL_FORCE=2.5`.
2. **Slow whirl**: `INWARD_PULL=0.02, TANGENTIAL_FORCE=0.8, DRAG=0.99`.
3. **Reverse rotation**: flip the sign of the tangential force.
4. **Galaxy palette**: change `PARTICLE_COLOUR` to a cooler shade, e.g. `(120, 180, 255)`, and `BG` to `(2, 2, 12)`.

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — what to expect">

The radial pull dominates. Particles collapse toward the centre quickly and the spiral becomes a tight, almost dot-like cluster after about 30 frames. Higher tangential force compensates partially but the dominant motion is inward.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — what to expect">

A wide, slow swirl that takes many seconds to develop. With lower drag the system retains energy and orbits decay much more slowly. Closer to a hurricane spiralling in over a day than a bathtub draining.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — what to expect">

`TANGENTIAL_FORCE = -1.7` (or flip the sign of the tangential unit vector). The vortex spins clockwise instead of counter-clockwise. Physically identical — the choice of rotation sense is a mirror reflection.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 4 — what to expect">

Visual identity entirely. Same physics, completely different vibe. Colour does more pedagogical work than most code changes — try `(255, 80, 60)` for fire, `(255, 240, 200)` for a sun, `(140, 220, 180)` for an aurora.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

**Implement**

1. **TODO 1**: `unit_tangential` — a 90° counter-clockwise rotation of `unit_radial`. For `(x, y)` the rotated vector is `(-y, x)`.
2. **TODO 2**: `radial_force = -INWARD_PULL * offsets` and `tangential_force = TANGENTIAL_FORCE * unit_tangential / np.sqrt(distances)`.

<Dropdown summary="Hint — the rotation in NumPy">

```python
unit_tangential = np.column_stack([-unit_radial[:, 1], unit_radial[:, 0]])
```

`unit_radial[:, 0]` is all the x-components; `unit_radial[:, 1]` is all the y-components. The rotation takes `(x, y) → (-y, x)`, so the new x-column is `-y` and the new y-column is `x`.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Hint — the falloff term">

`tangential_force = TANGENTIAL_FORCE * unit_tangential / np.sqrt(distances)`. The `1 / sqrt(distance)` term makes inner particles spin faster, which is what creates the "tight heart" of the vortex. Try `1 / distance` and `1 / (distance ** 2)` later to feel the difference.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

```python
unit_tangential = np.column_stack([-unit_radial[:, 1], unit_radial[:, 0]])
radial_force = -INWARD_PULL * offsets
tangential_force = TANGENTIAL_FORCE * unit_tangential / np.sqrt(distances)
```

</Dropdown>

- **Time-varying pull**: vary `INWARD_PULL` sinusoidally with frame number — the vortex breathes.
- **Colour by speed**: tint each particle's brightness by `|velocity|`. The vortex now visualises its own kinetic field.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

A vortex is a radial force plus a tangential force, applied per particle, every frame. The tangential is just the radial rotated by 90 degrees — `(-y, x)` — which means the entire "spiral" motion is one line of NumPy. Drag is what keeps the system stable; canvas-fading is what gives you trails for free without storing per-particle history. The same force-decomposition pattern — split a 2D motion into radial and tangential — recurs in orbital mechanics, magnetic fields, and steering behaviours for the rest of this module.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Forgetting the epsilon when dividing by distance. Particles that land on the centre crash the simulation.
- Skipping drag. Energy accumulates and particles fly off the canvas within seconds.
- Confusing the rotation `(-y, x)` (counter-clockwise) with `(y, -x)` (clockwise). The vortex spins the opposite way — easy to mis-debug as "force is too weak".
- Setting `TRAIL_FADE = 1.0`. The canvas never clears and the whole image saturates within a few frames.
- Using one global "speed limit" but per-particle velocities. Forgetting the `[over]` index turns every fast particle into a near-zero one.
