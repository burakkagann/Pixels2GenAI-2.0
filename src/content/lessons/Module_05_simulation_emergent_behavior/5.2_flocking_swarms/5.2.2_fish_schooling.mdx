## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

Real fish schools are tighter, faster, and more sharply polarised than the boids model predicts. The reason is biological: a fish responding to a too-close neighbour does not also try to *align* with that neighbour — it just flees. Iain Couzin and collaborators captured this in 2002 with a **zonal model**: three concentric annuli around each agent, with a strict priority order [1]. If anyone is in the **zone of repulsion**, only repulsion acts. Otherwise, the agent splits its attention between the **zone of orientation** (alignment) and the **zone of attraction** (cohesion). The change from "weighted sum of three rules" to "first non-empty zone wins" produces visibly sharper polarised motion — and it is the model now used in nearly all biological flocking literature.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Model an agent's neighbours as belonging to three concentric distance bands, not one perception radius.</li>
  <li>Implement priority-based rule selection: repulsion overrides everything; otherwise alignment + cohesion combine.</li>
  <li>Use a turn-rate parameter to smoothly interpolate between current and desired velocity — a critical fix for jittery flocks.</li>
  <li>Distinguish between **swarm** (no alignment), **polarised school** (strong alignment), and **mill** (orbiting cluster) regimes.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — three zones, one rule <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
for i in range(n):
    rep_mask = dists[i] < ZONE_REPULSION
    if rep_mask.any():
        desired[i] = normalize(-deltas[i, rep_mask].sum(axis=0)) * MAX_SPEED
        continue
    orient_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_REPULSION) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ORIENT)
    attract_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_ORIENT) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ATTRACT)
    align = velocities[orient_mask].sum(axis=0)
    attract = positions[attract_mask].mean(axis=0) - positions[i]
    desired[i] = normalize(align + 0.7 * attract) * MAX_SPEED
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Three concentric zones

Each fish has three distance bands around it. They are concentric circles in 2D; in 3D, concentric spheres. The exact radii depend on body length and water clarity for the species being modelled.

| Zone               | Range        | Active rule                           |
| ------------------ | ------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **Repulsion**      | `0 → 12`     | Steer **away** from the closest hits  |
| **Orientation**    | `12 → 32`    | Match average heading of neighbours   |
| **Attraction**     | `32 → 70`    | Steer toward centre of neighbours     |
| (Outside)          | `> 70`       | No interaction                        |

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="zones_demo.py">
```python
deltas = positions[:, None, :] - positions[None, :, :]
dists  = np.linalg.norm(deltas, axis=-1)
np.fill_diagonal(dists, np.inf)
```
</CodeBlock>

The `[:, None, :]` reshape pattern lets NumPy broadcast every pairwise difference in one step. `dists[i, j]` is the distance from fish `i` to fish `j`; `fill_diagonal(..., inf)` keeps each fish from counting itself as a neighbour.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Couzin's parameter sweep">
Couzin et al. (2002) ran a parameter sweep over the three zone widths and discovered that small changes can flip the school between three macro-regimes: *swarm* (no alignment), *polarised* (tight stream), and *mill* (orbiting torus). The biological implication: a single school can morph between behaviours by adjusting the *zone of orientation*, which is exactly what real schools do when threatened [1].
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Priority, not weighted sum

The boids algorithm sums separation, alignment, and cohesion with weights. The zonal model is *first non-empty zone wins*. If any neighbour is in the repulsion zone, only repulsion acts — alignment and cohesion are skipped entirely. Otherwise, alignment and cohesion combine.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="priority_demo.py">
```python
if rep_mask.any():
    desired[i] = normalize(-deltas[i, rep_mask].sum(axis=0)) * MAX_SPEED
    continue  # skip the rest entirely
```
</CodeBlock>

This is biologically motivated: a fish about to collide does not also "try to match heading". It just *moves*. The priority structure also makes the model robust against the parameter-balancing problem in boids — there is no "weights need re-tuning when density changes" issue.

<Admonition type="important">
A priority cascade is not a weighted sum. It is a discrete branch — entirely different behaviour mathematically. Replacing it with `0.7 * rep + 0.3 * (align + attract)` produces a different (worse) school. Resist the urge.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — Turn rate vs hard set

Setting the velocity directly to the "desired" direction makes the school flicker — fish snap between headings every frame. The fix is a turn-rate parameter `α ∈ (0, 1]` that smoothly interpolates:

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="turn_demo.py">
```python
velocities = velocities + TURN_RATE * (desired - velocities)
```
</CodeBlock>

With `TURN_RATE = 0.15`, only 15% of the difference between current and desired heading is applied per frame. The school responds smoothly, with realistic momentum. Set `TURN_RATE = 1.0` to recover the snap-instantly behaviour — useful to see how much of the "biological realism" comes from this single parameter.

<Admonition type="note">
The turn-rate parameter is the model's stand-in for fish body inertia, muscle response time, and limited sensory bandwidth — all collapsed into one knob. Real fish have a turning radius that is roughly proportional to body length; one fixed `TURN_RATE` is an approximation, but it is the right shape.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- The school visibly *crystallises* around frame 50–70. What is happening at that moment?
- What would happen if the orientation zone were empty for every fish?
- Why is the turn rate critical?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Crystallisation** — early frames have randomly-distributed fish whose neighbours are mostly in the attraction zone, so they slowly drift together. Once enough fish are inside one another's *orientation* zones, alignment forces start matching their velocities; the school transitions from a loose cloud to a polarised stream. That moment is a phase transition in the technical sense — the system suddenly has a coherent direction.

**No orientation zone** — set `ZONE_ORIENT = ZONE_REPULSION`. Fish still avoid each other and still try to cluster, but without alignment they form a vibrating ball rather than a stream. This is the *swarm* regime — like a cloud of midges over a pond, never going anywhere.

**Turn rate** — set `TURN_RATE = 1.0` and the school flickers chaotically. Each frame each fish snaps to a new heading and the school never stabilises. The turn rate is what couples the present state to future state; without it the simulation is memoryless.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Cross the regime boundary">

Edit the three zone widths in `fish_schooling.py`.

**Goals**

1. **Swarm regime**: `ZONE_ORIENT = 12.0` (kill orientation by making it the same as repulsion).
2. **Mill regime**: `ZONE_REPULSION = 6.0, ZONE_ORIENT = 60.0, ZONE_ATTRACT = 70.0`. Narrow attraction, wide orientation.
3. **Hyper-polarised**: `ZONE_ORIENT = 100.0`. Alignment dominates everything.
4. **Add a predator**: a single point that all fish flee within a 60-pixel "fear" radius, overriding the zones.

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — swarm">

Without orientation, fish never align headings. They cluster (attraction) and avoid (repulsion) but the school vibrates in place rather than moving as a unit. The midge-cloud look.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — mill">

With orientation wide and attraction narrow, the school orbits its own centre. Inner fish align with each other; outer fish are pulled inward. The result is the *torus* regime documented in real fish schools — a doughnut of moving bodies. This is what schools of barracuda do for hours at a time.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — hyper-polarised">

Alignment dominates: as soon as one fish has a heading, the whole school inherits it within a few frames. Looks like a marching band — too uniform to be biological, but a clean demonstration that orientation is the alignment knob.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 4 — predator">

Add a predator point that every fish steers directly away from when within 60 pixels:

```python
to_pred = positions[i] - predator_pos
d = np.linalg.norm(to_pred)
if d < 60:
    desired[i] = normalize(to_pred.reshape(1, 2))[0] * MAX_SPEED
    continue  # predator override
```

The school splits and reforms around the predator. Try a moving predator that follows the nearest fish.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Download href="/lesson-media/Module_05_simulation_emergent_behavior/5.2_flocking_swarms/5.2.2_fish_schooling/fish_starter.py" label="fish_starter.py — Exercise 3 scaffold" />

**Implement**

1. **TODO 1** — repulsion override: if any neighbour is inside `ZONE_REPULSION`, set `desired[i]` to point away from the sum of those repulsion vectors and `continue`.
2. **TODO 2** — orient + attract: otherwise, compute `align_dir` from the mean velocity of orientation-zone neighbours and `attract_dir` from `mean_position - my_position` of attraction-zone neighbours; the desired velocity is the normalised sum (with a 0.7 weight on attract).

<Dropdown summary="Hint 1 — masks">

```python
rep_mask = dists[i] < ZONE_REPULSION
orient_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_REPULSION) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ORIENT)
attract_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_ORIENT) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ATTRACT)
```

Each mask is a boolean array of length `n - 1` (after the self-distance was set to infinity). Use them to index into `velocities` and `positions` for averaging.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Hint 2 — repulsion direction">

```python
if rep_mask.any():
    away = -deltas[i, rep_mask]      # vectors from neighbours back to me
    desired[i] = normalize(away.sum(axis=0)) * MAX_SPEED
    continue
```

Note the minus sign: `deltas[i, j] = positions[i] - positions[j]` is the vector pointing **from j to i**. Its negative — what we want to flee — points from i toward j. We sum the negatives across all too-close neighbours, normalise, and that is the escape direction.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

```python
for i in range(n):
    rep_mask = dists[i] < ZONE_REPULSION
    if rep_mask.any():
        away = -deltas[i, rep_mask]
        desired[i] = normalize(away.sum(axis=0)) * MAX_SPEED
        continue
    orient_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_REPULSION) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ORIENT)
    attract_mask = (dists[i] >= ZONE_ORIENT) & (dists[i] < ZONE_ATTRACT)
    align_dir = velocities[orient_mask].sum(axis=0) if orient_mask.any() else np.zeros(2)
    attract_dir = (
        positions[attract_mask].mean(axis=0) - positions[i]
        if attract_mask.any() else np.zeros(2)
    )
    steer = align_dir + 0.7 * attract_dir
    if np.linalg.norm(steer) > 1e-3:
        desired[i] = normalize(steer.reshape(1, 2))[0] * MAX_SPEED
```

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Vision cone**: only count neighbours that are inside a 180° forward cone. Fish do not have eyes in the back of their heads.
- **Vary fish size**: bigger fish get bigger zones. Heterogeneous schools form sub-groups by size.
- **Light gradient**: bias `attract_dir` toward darker water. The school migrates as a unit.

</Exercise>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

The zonal model replaces boids's weighted sum with a strict priority cascade: repulsion overrides everything; otherwise alignment and attraction combine. Three concentric zones with priorities produces visibly sharper, more biological schools than weighted boids — a tighter group, a faster crystallisation, and clean regime transitions (swarm, polarised, mill) when you tune the zone widths. The turn-rate parameter is what couples present to future and makes the motion look like animal motion. Couzin's three-regime classification is now standard in biological flocking literature; you have just rebuilt the model in 60 lines.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Replacing the priority cascade with a weighted sum. The clean regime transitions disappear.
- Forgetting to fill the distance-matrix diagonal with infinity. Each fish counts itself as a neighbour and the system is biased toward "stay put".
- `TURN_RATE = 1.0`. The school chatters and never crystallises.
- Mixing up `deltas[i, j]` vs `deltas[j, i]`. The repulsion force points in the wrong direction and the school *attracts* in close range, immediately collapsing.
- Not normalising the desired velocity. Fish in dense regions sometimes get velocities of magnitude 30 from many overlapping zones.
