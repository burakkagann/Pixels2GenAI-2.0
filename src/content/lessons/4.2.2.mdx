## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

A lightning bolt is not a static fractal at all — it is the transient discharge channel of a stepped leader that travels from cloud to ground in microsecond-scale bursts. But the *shape* of the channel — jagged on every visible scale, occasionally forking, never quite straight — has the statistical signature of a self-similar curve [1]. That is enough to generate convincing bolts from one short recursive rule: **midpoint displacement**. Take a straight segment, split it in half, displace the midpoint perpendicular to the segment by a random amount, halve the displacement budget, recurse on the two new sub-segments. The output is the canonical 1D analogue of the diamond-square algorithm used for terrain (4.2.3) — same idea, applied to a polyline instead of a height map [2].

This lesson is short by design: the midpoint-displacement primitive is small, the parameters are few, and once you understand the **perpendicular offset** the rest is parameter-sweeping. The conceptual move is recognising that recursion does not always mean "divide and recurse on disjoint regions" (as in 4.1.5) — here it means "subdivide and refine the same object."

## Quick start — one bolt <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw

def subdivide(points, displacement, decay=0.5, depth=7, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    if depth == 0:
        return points
    new = [points[0]]
    for i in range(len(points) - 1):
        a, b = points[i], points[i + 1]
        mid = ((a[0] + b[0]) / 2, (a[1] + b[1]) / 2)
        dx, dy = b[0] - a[0], b[1] - a[1]
        length = (dx*dx + dy*dy) ** 0.5 or 1.0
        nx, ny = -dy / length, dx / length            # unit perpendicular
        off = rng.uniform(-displacement, displacement)
        new.append((mid[0] + nx * off, mid[1] + ny * off))
        new.append(b)
    return subdivide(new, displacement * decay, decay, depth - 1, rng)

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
pts = subdivide([(300, 30), (300, 770)], displacement=120, depth=8, rng=rng)

img = Image.new('RGB', (600, 800), (8, 10, 25))
ImageDraw.Draw(img).line(pts, fill=(180, 220, 255), width=2)
img.save('simple_lightning.png')
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Midpoint displacement on a polyline

Start with two endpoints $A$ and $B$. The midpoint is at $M = (A + B) / 2$. Push $M$ perpendicular to the segment $AB$ by a random offset; you now have three points: $A, M', B$. Apply the same construction to each of the two new segments, with a smaller displacement budget. Repeat.

The first observation: **the perturbation must be perpendicular**. If you push the midpoint along the segment, you just shift it — no jaggedness appears. The perpendicular is the direction in which displacement creates new geometry. For an edge $(dx, dy)$, the unit perpendicular is $(-dy, dx)$ divided by the segment length — rotate the edge vector 90° and normalise.

The second observation: **the displacement budget halves at every recursion**. If the budget didn't shrink, every level would add equally large jitter and the bolt would look like noise. Halving ensures large-scale features come from the first few levels and fine detail comes from the last few — exactly what we want.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know · the same recipe drives Brownian motion">
A Brownian-motion trajectory can be reconstructed by midpoint displacement with a specific decay exponent — for **fractional Brownian motion** (fBm) the decay at each level is two raised to the power minus the Hurst exponent $H$, where $H$ is a real number in $(0, 1)$. The lightning bolt's `decay = 0.5` corresponds to $H = 1$, the smoothest end of the family. Lower decays give "rougher" motion with smaller Hurst exponents [2].
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Roughness and the decay parameter

The `decay` parameter is the single knob that controls how rough the bolt looks. The relationship between `decay` and the apparent fractal dimension is:

> $D \approx 2 + \log_2$(decay).

- `decay = 0.5` (the default) → $D \approx 1$. The bolt is **as smooth as it can be while still being self-similar**. This is the Brownian/Hurst-1 endpoint.
- `decay = 0.4` → $D \approx 1.68$. Distinctly rougher; the bolt zigzags at every scale.
- `decay = 0.6` → $D \approx 0.74$. Smoother than Brownian — small-scale perturbations decay below visibility.
- `decay = 0.75` → $D \approx 0.42$. Barely fractal at all; the bolt is essentially a randomly displaced straight line.

### Concept 3 — Branching with probability

Real lightning forks. Add forks by visiting the bolt's midpoints after the main subdivision, flipping a coin at each, and spawning a smaller, dimmer bolt branching off at a random angle:

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="branching.py">
```python
def draw_bolt(draw, start, end, displacement, depth, rng,
              brightness=255, branch_prob=0.3):

```
</CodeBlock>

Each child bolt is itself a recursive midpoint-displacement bolt, with a smaller displacement budget, shallower recursion, and dimmer colour. The `brightness < 80` short-circuit stops the recursion before it becomes visually pointless.

<Admonition type="important">
The branching code is **not the midpoint algorithm in disguise**. Midpoint displacement adds jaggedness *within* an existing polyline by moving its vertices. Branching adds *new* polylines anchored at the parent's vertices. They are two separate fractal operations applied in sequence.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- How many vertices does the polyline have after 8 levels of subdivision?
- Why is the perpendicular direction $(-dy, dx)$ normalised by the segment length, rather than $(dy, -dx)$ normalised by the same length?
- What happens visually if you set `decay = 1.0`?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Vertex count** — start with 2 points and 1 edge. Each subdivision inserts 1 new point per edge and doubles the edge count. After $n$ subdivisions you have $2^n + 1$ points and $2^n$ edges. At depth 8: 257 points, 256 edges. (Off by one from `2^8` — the +1 is the starting vertex.)

**Why $(-dy, dx)$** — both `(-dy, dx)` and `(dy, -dx)` are unit perpendiculars, pointing in opposite directions. Since the offset is uniform in the interval from minus-displacement to plus-displacement — symmetric around zero — the sign of the perpendicular doesn't matter. Either convention produces statistically identical bolts.

**`decay = 1.0`** — every recursion uses the same displacement budget. The bolt becomes pure noise at every scale: the small-scale wiggles are just as wild as the large-scale arc, so the silhouette stops looking like lightning and starts looking like random scatter.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Midpoint displacement subdivides a polyline by inserting a new midpoint between every pair of vertices and offsetting it perpendicular to the original edge by a random amount; the displacement budget halves at every level. One knob — `decay` — controls the bolt's apparent roughness via $D \approx 2 + \log_2$ of decay. Branching is a second, independent fractal operation that spawns smaller child bolts at random midpoints with probability `branch_prob`. This is the 1D version of the diamond-square heightmap algorithm in 4.2.3 — same primitive, different topology. Once the recursion structure clicks, replacing the midpoint with any other "average + perturb" rule generalises to coastlines, river networks, and crack patterns.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- **Wrong perpendicular sign.** $(-dy, dx)$ or $(dy, -dx)$ are both fine; mixing them within a single call produces lopsided bolts.
- **Forgetting to scale the perpendicular by `1 / length`.** The perturbation magnitude becomes proportional to segment length and the bolt blows up uncontrollably.
- **Decay > 0.7 looks too smooth.** Visually it stops being recognisable as lightning. Keep `decay` in `[0.4, 0.6]` for the classic look.
- **Infinite branching recursion.** Always decay `brightness` and depth in the child call, and short-circuit at `brightness < 80`. Without the brake, branches branch indefinitely.
