## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

Real ants solve a hard combinatorial problem every day: find the shortest path from the nest to a food source. They do it without any global plan and without remembering anything individually. Each ant lays pheromone on the trail it walked; subsequent ants are more likely to follow strong pheromone trails; short trails get more passes per minute and so accumulate more pheromone than long trails. The colony as a whole converges on near-optimal routes. Marco Dorigo formalised this into **Ant Colony Optimisation** (ACO) in 1992 and applied it to the travelling-salesman problem [1]. In this lesson you will reproduce his algorithm: a graph of cities, virtual ants probabilistically choosing the next city based on pheromone and visibility, and a per-iteration evaporate-and-deposit step. You will watch the strongest edges crystallise as a near-optimal tour over roughly 60 iterations.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Recognise the travelling-salesman problem and why brute force is hopeless past about 12 cities.</li>
  <li>Implement probabilistic city selection using the score `pheromone^α × visibility^β`.</li>
  <li>Apply pheromone evaporation as a forgetting mechanism that lets the colony escape bad early choices.</li>
  <li>Distinguish exploration (uniform-ish probabilities, high β = 0) from exploitation (sharp probabilities, high β = 5).</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — one ant, one tour <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
def build_tour(distances, pheromone, rng):
    n = distances.shape[0]
    current = rng.integers(0, n)
    visited = [current]
    while len(visited) < n:
        mask = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
        mask[visited] = False
        scores = (pheromone[current] ** ALPHA) * ((1 / distances[current]) ** BETA)
        scores[~mask] = 0
        scores = scores / scores.sum()
        current = int(rng.choice(n, p=scores))
        visited.append(current)
    return visited
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — TSP and why heuristics matter

The travelling-salesman problem asks: given N cities, find the shortest closed tour that visits each city exactly once. There are `(N-1)!/2` distinct tours for N cities — that is 10⁵ for 10 cities, but 10¹⁸ for 21 cities. Exact algorithms (branch-and-bound, dynamic programming) crawl past N≈30. We do not want exact; we want **good enough, quickly**. ACO is one of several *metaheuristics* that trade optimality for speed [2].

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Real ant experiments">
Jean-Louis Deneubourg's 1990 binary-bridge experiment placed an ant colony at one end of a Y-shaped bridge with a long arm and a short arm leading to food. The colony converged on the short arm within an hour — entirely through pheromone reinforcement, with no spatial perception of length [3]. ACO is the computational mirror of that experiment.
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Probabilistic city selection

At each step of a tour, the ant at city `i` decides where to go next. The probability of choosing city `j` is

`P(j) ∝ τ(i,j)^α · η(i,j)^β`

where `τ(i,j)` is the pheromone level on edge `(i, j)` and `η(i,j) = 1/distance(i,j)` is the visibility. The exponents `α` and `β` tune the trade-off:

- **α = 0** — pheromone ignored, ants make distance-greedy choices (nearest-neighbour).
- **β = 0** — distance ignored, ants follow pheromone blindly.
- **α = 1, β = 4** — a typical balance. Distance dominates early, pheromone takes over once trails build up.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="probabilistic_demo.py">
```python
scores = (pheromone[current] ** ALPHA) * ((1.0 / distances[current]) ** BETA)
scores[~unvisited_mask] = 0
probs = scores / scores.sum()
next_city = rng.choice(n, p=probs)
```
</CodeBlock>

The choice is *probabilistic*, not greedy. Even with strong pheromone on one edge, occasionally an ant explores a weaker alternative. That occasional exploration is what lets the colony escape locally-good-but-globally-bad tours.

<Admonition type="important">
Set `β = 0` and the algorithm becomes pure pheromone-following. Set `α = 0` and it becomes nearest-neighbour. The interesting behaviour lives in between, and that interesting behaviour is the whole point.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — Evaporation + deposit

After all ants have built their tours, the pheromone map updates in two stages.

**Evaporation** — multiply every pheromone value by `(1 - ρ)` where `ρ ∈ (0, 1]` is the evaporation rate. This is forgetting: edges that nobody is using fade away.

**Deposit** — every ant adds `Q / tour_length` to each edge in its tour. Shorter tours deposit more per edge; longer tours deposit less. The best ants vote loudest.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="evaporation_demo.py">
```python
pheromone *= (1 - EVAPORATION)
for tour, L in zip(tours, lengths):
    deposit = Q / L
    for k in range(N):
        a, b = tour[k], tour[(k + 1) % N]
        pheromone[a, b] += deposit
        pheromone[b, a] += deposit
```
</CodeBlock>

Evaporation is crucial. Without it, every edge that any ant ever used accumulates pheromone forever, and the algorithm gets stuck on whatever was best in the first few iterations. With it, only edges that *continue to be useful* keep accumulating.

<Admonition type="note">
The evaporation rate `ρ` is the algorithm's exploration knob. High `ρ` ≈ 0.5 → fast forgetting, lots of re-exploration, slow convergence. Low `ρ` ≈ 0.05 → slow forgetting, fast convergence, more likely to get stuck.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- The best tour stops improving around iteration 25 but the pheromone field keeps changing. What is the field doing in that latter half?
- Why do all ants start at random cities, not the same one?
- What happens at iteration 0, when all pheromone is uniform?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Late-iteration pheromone changes** — once the best tour is locked in, the deposit step reinforces those edges, while evaporation eats away at all the others. The visible "convergence" is the high-contrast emergence of those reinforced edges; the algorithm has not improved the tour, but it has cleaned up the visual signal.

**Random starting cities** — symmetric exploration. If every ant started at city 0, the first-step decisions would all be biased toward whatever edges leave city 0. Random starts mean every city participates equally in early-iteration probability mass.

**Iteration 0** — uniform pheromone makes the choice depend entirely on visibility. The first iteration is essentially nearest-neighbour for every ant, biased by where each one started. The pheromone signal builds from there.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Cross the exploration / exploitation boundary">

Edit the CONFIG block of `ant_colony.py`.

**Goals**

1. **Pure pheromone-following**: `ALPHA = 1.0, BETA = 0.0`. Distance ignored entirely.
2. **Pure greedy distance**: `ALPHA = 0.0, BETA = 4.0`. Pheromone ignored.
3. **No memory**: `EVAPORATION = 1.0` (everything forgotten each iter).
4. **Permanent memory**: `EVAPORATION = 0.0` (nothing ever forgotten).

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — pure pheromone">

The colony locks onto whatever tour first emerges and never escapes it. With nothing reining in pheromone build-up by edge quality, all the early-iteration edges accumulate. Final tour length is typically 20–40% worse than the canonical balance.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — pure greedy">

Every ant follows nearest-neighbour from its random starting city. The colony finds a tour that depends heavily on the starting city distribution — sometimes 5% worse than optimal, sometimes 30% worse. No actual learning happens.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — no memory">

Every iteration starts from uniform pheromone. No reinforcement learning. The final result is the best of ~80 independent "nearest-neighbour + slight randomness" runs — surprisingly competitive on small problems, but it never gets better with more iterations.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 4 — permanent memory">

Pheromone accumulates without bound. Whatever edges look good in the first 5 iterations get reinforced forever; the colony gets stuck in a local optimum within 10 iterations and never moves. Watch the GIF: the best-tour line stops changing very early.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

total = scores.sum()

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

```python
def build_tour(distances, pheromone, rng):
    n = distances.shape[0]
    current = rng.integers(0, n)
    visited = [current]
    while len(visited) < n:
        mask = np.ones(n, dtype=bool)
        mask[visited] = False
        scores = (pheromone[current] ** ALPHA) * ((1.0 / distances[current]) ** BETA)
        scores[~mask] = 0
        total = scores.sum()
        if total <= 0:
            current = int(rng.choice(np.where(mask)[0]))
        else:
            current = int(rng.choice(n, p=scores / total))
        visited.append(current)
    return visited
```

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Elitist deposit**: after the standard deposit, give the *best* tour a 5× bonus deposit. Faster convergence at the cost of more local-optimum risk.
- **MMAS clamping**: clamp pheromone to `[τ_min, τ_max]` to prevent any edge from dominating. This is *Max-Min Ant System* [4].
- **Plot the best tour length over iterations**. The curve is shaped like a hockey stick — sharp early, then a long flat tail.

</Exercise>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Ant Colony Optimisation solves combinatorial problems by simulating pheromone-based stigmergy. Each iteration: ants build probabilistic tours weighted by `pheromone^α × visibility^β`, then evaporation eats away unused pheromone and the best tours deposit fresh pheromone on their edges. The colony's collective memory lives entirely in the pheromone matrix; no individual ant remembers anything. ACO will not beat specialised TSP solvers on huge instances, but on irregular graphs, dynamic costs, and problems with hard-to-encode constraints, the metaheuristic shines.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Skipping evaporation. The colony locks into early decisions and never improves.
- `BETA = 0`. The algorithm becomes pure pheromone-following with no distance awareness; tours look erratic.
- Forgetting to symmetrise pheromone deposits. `pheromone[a, b]` and `pheromone[b, a]` must both update — TSP is symmetric.
- Initialising pheromone at zero. First-iteration scores are all zero and the algorithm crashes on `nan` probabilities. Always start at 1.
- Using `np.random.choice` with `p=` and tiny floating-point sums. The library rounds and rejects. Renormalise just before sampling.
