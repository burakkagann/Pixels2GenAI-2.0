## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

Motion detection (11.2.1) tells you *that* something moved. Optical flow tells you *which way* and *how fast*. For every pixel (or sparse grid of pixels) in a frame pair, optical flow estimates a 2D motion vector $(u, v)$ — the displacement that maps a feature in frame $t$ to its corresponding feature in frame $t + 1$. The output is a dense vector field over the image.

Berthold Horn and Brian Schunck published the first practical algorithm in 1981 [1]; Bruce Lucas and Takeo Kanade independently published a sparse, gradient-based variant the same year [2]. Both rest on the **brightness-constancy assumption**: a moving object keeps the same colour from one frame to the next. From that single assumption plus a 2D Taylor expansion, you get the optical-flow equation. Every modern variant — Farnebäck dense flow, RAFT neural flow, PWC-Net — is a refinement of the same idea.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">

  <li>Render the flow field as arrows overlaid on the source frame.</li>
  <li>Recognise the dense (Horn-Schunck), sparse (Lucas-Kanade), and modern neural (RAFT) variants as different ways to solve the same constraint.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — sparse flow arrows <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
import numpy as np

def lucas_kanade_flow(prev, nxt, window=8, step=16):
    prev_f = prev.astype(np.float32)
    nxt_f = nxt.astype(np.float32)
    Ix = np.gradient(prev_f, axis=1)
    Iy = np.gradient(prev_f, axis=0)
    It = nxt_f - prev_f

    H, W = prev.shape
    flow = []
    for y in range(window, H - window, step):
        for x in range(window, W - window, step):
            sl = (slice(y-window, y+window+1), slice(x-window, x+window+1))
            A = np.stack([Ix[sl].ravel(), Iy[sl].ravel()], axis=1)
            b = -It[sl].ravel()
            uv, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)
            flow.append((x, y, uv[0], uv[1]))
    return flow
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Brightness constancy + Taylor expansion

The brightness-constancy assumption: a moving feature retains its colour from one frame to the next. If $I(x, y, t)$ is intensity at position $(x, y)$ time $t$, then:

`I(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t)`

where $(u, v)$ is the displacement. First-order Taylor expansion around $(x, y, t)$ gives:

`I + I_x * u + I_y * v + I_t = I`

Cancel $I$ from both sides and you have the **optical-flow constraint**:

`I_x * u + I_y * v + I_t = 0`

One equation, two unknowns — under-determined per pixel. You need a second constraint to solve. Lucas-Kanade gets it from assuming **constant flow over a small neighbourhood**; Horn-Schunck gets it from a **smoothness regulariser** on the entire image.

### Concept 2 — Lucas-Kanade: local least squares

Assume the flow $(u, v)$ is the same for every pixel in a small window (typically 5×5 to 21×21). Each pixel in the window contributes one optical-flow equation; together they form an over-determined linear system:

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="ls_system.py">
```python
A = np.stack([Ix_window.ravel(), Iy_window.ravel()], axis=1)   # (N, 2)
b = -It_window.ravel()                                          # (N,)
uv, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)                      # (2,)
u, v = uv
```
</CodeBlock>

`np.linalg.lstsq` solves the least-squares problem: minimise the squared residual norm $\|A \cdot uv - b\|^2$ over the unknown $(u, v)$. The window size trades **localisation** (smaller = better at picking up sharp boundaries) against **noise robustness** (larger = more equations, more averaging).

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know · optical flow drives every video codec">
H.264, H.265, AV1, and VP9 video codecs all use motion estimation at their core: compute the optical flow between consecutive frames, encode the flow vectors plus the residual error rather than the full frames. A 1080p video at 30 fps compressed to 5 Mbps is 95% motion vectors and 5% pixel data. Same algorithm underlies every video service from YouTube to FaceTime to Netflix. The codec spec doesn't mandate which flow algorithm to use — choosing a good one is the main competitive axis between codec vendors [3].
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — Dense vs. sparse, classical vs. neural

The classical taxonomy of optical flow:

- **Sparse** (Lucas-Kanade) — solve at a grid of points or at corner features (Shi-Tomasi). Fast, robust at corners, poor on smooth regions.
- **Dense** (Horn-Schunck, Farnebäck) — solve per-pixel with a smoothness regulariser. Slower, but produces a flow vector at every pixel.

Modern neural networks (FlowNet 2015, PWC-Net 2018, RAFT 2020 [4]) replace the classical solver with a learned model. RAFT in particular achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on the Sintel benchmark and is the default choice in academic research today. The classical algorithms remain the right choice when you need sub-millisecond latency or when you can't ship a model.

<Admonition type="important">
The brightness-constancy assumption fails wherever **lighting changes** (a face turning under directional light), **occlusion** happens (one object hides another), or **textureless regions** exist (white wall — no gradient to lock onto). All three are open research problems. For installation art the failures are usually visible but acceptable; for self-driving or robotic perception they need to be handled with multi-frame fusion and learned priors.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- The wall arrows point right. Why don't the disc arrows also point right?
- What does the flow look like on a flat-coloured background (zero gradient)?
- The window size is 8 (so a 17×17 window). What happens with `window = 2`?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Disc arrows are rotational** — the disc is *rotating*, not translating. Each disc pixel's local motion is a tangential velocity around the centre. The algorithm picks this up because the spoke pattern shifts angularly rather than linearly between frames.

**Zero-gradient region** — the LK least-squares system becomes degenerate ($A$ has rank 0). No solution — the algorithm shows no flow there. You see this as gaps in the arrow field on uniform regions. This is the **aperture problem** in disguise.

**Tiny window** — `window = 2` gives a 5×5 LS system, very noisy. Arrows scatter randomly. Larger windows over-smooth at object boundaries but produce stable estimates inside objects. 8–10 is the sweet spot for most natural scenes.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Sweep flow parameters">

**Goals**

1. **Denser grid.** Drop `step` from 16 to 6. The arrow field becomes denser; cost grows as $N^2$.
2. **Larger window.** Increase `window` to 16. The flow becomes smoother but loses detail at object boundaries.
3. **Magnitude threshold.** Filter arrows with magnitude below 1 pixel — only show "significant" motion.

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — what to expect">

Denser arrows, especially visible on the disc rim. Runtime grows quickly — at step=2 you'd be solving 10,000+ least-squares systems per frame.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — what to expect">

The flow field smooths out — boundary effects shrink. But fine motion details (the disc's spokes turning) get averaged with the surrounding stationary regions. Trade-off.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — what to expect">

Static or near-static regions become arrow-free; only the wall and disc show motion. Useful for highlighting where the *interesting* motion is.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

```python
def motion_trail(frame, flow_points, trail_len=10):
    trail = frame.copy().astype(np.float32)
    H, W = frame.shape[:2]
    for x, y, u, v in flow_points:
        color = frame[y, x].astype(np.float32)
        for k in range(1, trail_len + 1):
            tx = int(x - u * k * 0.5)
            ty = int(y - v * k * 0.5)
            if 0 <= tx < W and 0 <= ty < H:
                fade = 1 - k / trail_len
                trail[ty, tx] = trail[ty, tx] * (1 - 0.3 * fade) + color * 0.3 * fade
    return trail.clip(0, 255).astype(np.uint8)
```

For each tracked point, walk backwards along its flow vector and blend the centre colour into a fading trail. Long exposures become particle trails.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

The motion-trail effect on the disc produces curving trails behind each spoke; on the wall it produces uniform horizontal streaks. Same visual primitive that drives long-exposure photography ("light painting" smartphone modes) and slow-motion video stylization.

</Dropdown>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Optical flow estimates per-pixel motion vectors $(u, v)$ between two frames. The brightness-constancy assumption plus a 1st-order Taylor expansion gives one equation per pixel; Lucas-Kanade adds a "constant flow in a local window" assumption to close the system; Horn-Schunck uses a smoothness regulariser instead. Modern variants are neural (RAFT) but they solve the same constraint. The output drives every video codec, every slow-motion interpolation, every stabilisation app, and many tracking and gesture-recognition pipelines. Failures occur at zero-gradient regions, lighting changes, and occlusions — all open research problems.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- **Trying to compute flow on uniform-colour regions.** The LK system is singular there; no solution.
- **Tiny windows.** Noisy estimates; the visualization scatters randomly.
- **Forgetting the brightness-constancy failure modes.** Lighting changes, specular highlights, and shadows all break the assumption.
- **Using sparse LK where you need dense Farnebäck.** Boundary pixels get no estimate.
