## Big Question — *what makes one separating line better than another?* <a id="big-question"></a>

If two classes of points are linearly separable in 2D, infinitely many straight lines can separate them. **Support Vector Machines**, introduced by Vapnik and colleagues at AT&T Bell Labs in the late 1980s and formalised in the 1995 paper [1], pick the line whose perpendicular distance to the nearest training point of either class — the **margin** — is as large as possible. The intuition is robustness: a small-margin boundary nearly touches the data, so a tiny perturbation could flip its classification; a large-margin boundary has slack. Statistical learning theory makes this precise — generalisation error is bounded by a function that *decreases* as the margin grows, independent of the dimensionality of the feature space [2].

Then the **kernel trick** (Aizerman, Braverman, Rozonoer 1964; Boser, Guyon, Vapnik 1992 [3]) takes the linear maximum-margin classifier and lets it draw curved boundaries — without ever computing the curve in coordinates. Replace the dot product `⟨x, y⟩` with a *kernel function* `K(x, y)` that secretly computes the dot product in a higher-dimensional space, and the same SVM equations now find the maximum margin hyperplane in that space. Project back to 2D and the hyperplane looks like a curve.

## Part 1 — Maximum margin and support vectors <a id="part-1"></a>

The decision boundary of a binary linear SVM is the hyperplane `w · x + b = 0`. The classifier outputs `sign(w · x + b)`. Among all hyperplanes that classify all training points correctly, the SVM picks the one that maximises `2 / ‖w‖`, the perpendicular distance between the two parallel "margin" planes `w · x + b = ±1` that just touch the nearest training points.

The training points sitting exactly on those margin planes — the ones at perpendicular distance `1 / ‖w‖` from the boundary — are the **support vectors**. The trained classifier only depends on them; remove any non-support training point and the boundary does not move. This is one of the most striking properties of SVMs: a trained model on 10,000 points might depend on only a handful of them.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="margin_demo.py">
```python
clf = SVC(kernel='linear', C=1.0).fit(X, y)
# clf.support_vectors_   -> the support vector positions
# clf.coef_, clf.intercept_  -> w and b for the boundary
# margin = 2.0 / np.linalg.norm(clf.coef_)
```
</CodeBlock>

For real-world data that is not linearly separable, the SVM relaxes "all correct" to "soft margin": each violating point pays a penalty proportional to how far it is on the wrong side. The hyperparameter `C` trades off margin width vs. penalty: high `C` means "do not violate", low `C` means "wider margin even at the cost of misclassification". `C` is the SVM's single most important knob [4].

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Vapnik and statistical learning theory">
Vladimir Vapnik developed Statistical Learning Theory throughout the 1970s and 80s in the Soviet Union, working out exactly when an empirical risk minimiser generalises. The SVM emerged from that framework: it is the algorithm that *constructively* achieves the generalisation bound. Bell Labs hired him in 1990; the 1995 paper was his first major Western publication. Statistical Learning Theory is still the cleanest theoretical foundation in all of ML — predates neural-network theory by decades [1, 2].
</Admonition>

## Part 2 — The kernel trick <a id="part-2"></a>

Linear SVMs only handle linearly separable problems. To handle curved boundaries, you could explicitly project your data into a higher dimension (e.g. add squared and cross-product features) and train a linear SVM there. For polynomial degree-2 features in 2D, you go from 2 features to 6 — manageable. For degree-3 in 100 features, you go from 100 features to 176,851 — not manageable. For infinite-dimensional projections, never possible.

The kernel trick observes that the SVM's training and prediction equations only ever access the data through *dot products* `⟨x, y⟩`. Replace those dot products with a **kernel function** `K(x, y)` that satisfies Mercer's condition — symmetric, positive semi-definite — and you are equivalently working in a higher-dimensional space where the kernel *is* a dot product, even though you never actually compute the high-dimensional coordinates.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="rbf_demo.py">
```python
def rbf_kernel(X1, X2, gamma):
    # K(x, y) = exp(-gamma * ||x - y||^2)
    sq = ((X1[:, None] - X2[None, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=-1)
    return np.exp(-gamma * sq)
```
</CodeBlock>

The **RBF kernel** `K(x, y) = exp(-γ ||x - y||²)` secretly works in an *infinite-dimensional* space — the Taylor series of the exponential gives infinitely many polynomial features. The SVM in that space has an arbitrarily complex decision boundary; the gamma parameter controls how "tight" each support vector's influence is. Small gamma → smooth global boundary; large gamma → tight bubbles around each support vector (the overfitting case in the bottom-right of Figure 1).

<Admonition type="important">
The kernel trick is one of the deepest ideas in classical ML and recurs everywhere. Gaussian process regression *is* kernel regression. Kernel PCA is PCA in feature space. Neural-tangent-kernel theory shows that wide neural networks behave like kernel methods in a specific limit [5]. Once you have seen the trick, you keep recognising it.
</Admonition>

## Part 3 — Kernel zoo: linear, polynomial, RBF <a id="part-3"></a>

The choice of kernel determines the boundary shape and is the SVM's primary architectural decision.

- **Linear** — `K(x, y) = ⟨x, y⟩`. Gives a flat hyperplane. Use when features are already engineered to be roughly linear, or in very high dimensions (text classification with TF-IDF features, where the dimension is large enough that linear is already expressive).
- **Polynomial** — `K(x, y) = (⟨x, y⟩ + c)^d`. Gives a smooth polynomial boundary of degree `d`. Useful for problems where the "right" boundary is some smooth curve.
- **RBF / Gaussian** — `K(x, y) = exp(-γ ||x - y||²)`. The local-bubble kernel, infinite-dimensional. The default kernel for unknown structure.
- **Custom** — string kernels for text, graph kernels for molecules, image-patch kernels for vision. Any positive-semi-definite similarity function will do.

Picking γ and `C` for RBF SVM is the most common practical tuning problem. The standard approach is grid search over `(C, γ) ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} × {scale, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}` with cross-validation [4].

**Reflection questions**

- In the RBF γ=10 panel, almost every training point is a support vector. What does that tell you about the model's complexity and likely test error?
- The linear SVM cannot separate the two crescents — accuracy is well below 1.0. Why doesn't a higher `C` fix this?
- Why is the kernel trick useless for *very* high-dimensional input data (say 100,000 features)?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**γ=10 overfits** — when every training point is a support vector, the model has essentially memorised the data. Train accuracy approaches 1.0 because the bubble around each point covers itself; test accuracy collapses because the bubbles do not extend to similar points. The model has high variance — the canonical overfitting signature.

**C cannot rescue linear** — increasing `C` makes the SVM less tolerant of margin violations, but it still has to draw a straight line. No straight line separates two interlocking crescents. The fix is a different kernel, not more `C`. `C` controls how strict you are *given* a fixed model class; it does not change the class.

**Kernel trick at 100k features** — the kernel matrix is `n × n`. Computing it requires `n² × d` operations. With `n = 10,000` and `d = 100,000` that is 10¹³ operations per fit — 30 minutes on a CPU. Worse, the implicit feature space is much higher dimensional than the input, so generalisation suffers. For very high-D input, use a linear SVM (or, in 2026, a transformer) and rely on the input features already being expressive.

</Dropdown>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

The Support Vector Machine finds the maximum-margin hyperplane separating two classes, depending only on the support vectors that touch the margin. The kernel trick lifts the same algorithm into higher (sometimes infinite) dimensions by replacing dot products with positive-semi-definite kernel functions, producing curved decision boundaries without ever computing the high-D coordinates. The RBF kernel is the default when you do not know the structure; linear is the default in very high dimensions. SVMs reigned in image classification, document classification, and bioinformatics from 1995 to 2014, when deep neural networks took over for problems where data is plentiful and structure is rich. For small-to-medium tabular problems with limited data, SVMs remain a strong baseline in 2026.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Forgetting to standardise features. SVM distances depend on feature scaling; one feature with a huge range dominates the margin geometry.
- Using `C` and `gamma` defaults without cross-validation. The defaults are sensible but the optimal values can be orders of magnitude different.
- Training an RBF SVM on 100,000 points and waiting for it. SVM training is between `O(n²)` and `O(n³)`; past ~50,000 points use linear SVM or specialised solvers (LinearSVC, kernel approximation).
- Reading too much into "support vectors as important examples". They are *boundary* examples, not representative or prototypical examples. A support vector is "the data point closest to the decision rule", not "the most typical class member".
- Comparing SVM accuracy to neural networks on small datasets and dismissing the neural network because it underperforms. The comparison is honest only with sufficient data.
