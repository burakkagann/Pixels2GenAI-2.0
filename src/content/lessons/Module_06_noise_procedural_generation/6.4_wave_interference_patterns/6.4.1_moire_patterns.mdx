## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

**Moiré patterns** appear whenever two regular patterns overlap at a small offset — slightly different angles, slightly different scales, slightly displaced origins. The eye reads the *difference frequency* of the two patterns as a large-scale wavy pattern that has nothing to do with either source [1]. The same phenomenon causes the colour patterns on uncorrected TV screenshots, the strobing on photographs of textured fabrics, and the security stripes on bank notes. Mathematically it is the same idea as audio beating — two close frequencies generate a slow envelope at their difference. This lesson builds the visual moiré effect from two sinusoids in NumPy.

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Build a directional line pattern as `cos(2π · (x·cos θ + y·sin θ) / λ)` for any angle and wavelength.</li>
  <li>Sum two such patterns at slightly different angles to produce moiré beating.</li>
  <li>Predict the spacing of the moiré bands from the angle difference and source wavelength.</li>
  <li>Build concentric-ring moiré with two slightly-offset distance fields.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — two angled line patterns <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

H, W = 400, 400
WAVELENGTH = 6        # pixels per cycle
ANGLE_DEG = 5         # offset between the two patterns

y, x = np.indices((H, W)).astype(np.float64)

a = 0.0
b = np.deg2rad(ANGLE_DEG)
pattern_a = np.cos(2 * np.pi * (x * np.cos(a) + y * np.sin(a)) / WAVELENGTH)
pattern_b = np.cos(2 * np.pi * (x * np.cos(b) + y * np.sin(b)) / WAVELENGTH)

moire = (pattern_a + pattern_b)
moire = (moire - moire.min()) / (moire.max() - moire.min())

Image.fromarray((moire * 255).astype(np.uint8), 'L').save('moire_lines.png')
```
</CodeBlock>

<Admonition type="tip">
The beating is a *visual* artefact of summing two near-frequencies — your eye sees the slow envelope as a pattern of its own. In signal-processing terms, `cos(ω₁ x) + cos(ω₂ x) = 2 cos((ω₁ + ω₂)/2 · x) · cos((ω₁ − ω₂)/2 · x)`. The slow `cos((ω₁ − ω₂)/2 · x)` factor is the moiré envelope [2].
</Admonition>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — A directional line pattern

A regular set of parallel lines at angle `θ` and wavelength `λ` is the cosine of the projection of `(x, y)` onto the perpendicular direction:

```python
pattern = np.cos(2 * np.pi * (x * np.cos(θ) + y * np.sin(θ)) / λ)
```

- `x cos θ + y sin θ` is the projection of position onto direction `θ` — the same dot product from any "rotate the axis" computation.
- Dividing by `λ` scales so one wavelength corresponds to `2π` of phase.
- The cosine maps phase to a `[-1, 1]` value — bright stripes where the cosine is positive, dark where it's negative [3].

Changing `θ` rotates the stripes; changing `λ` controls their spacing. Two such patterns at almost-the-same angle produce moiré.

### Concept 2 — Why summing produces beating

Adding two cosines of similar frequency is the textbook example of *beats*. The trig identity:

```
cos(α) + cos(β) = 2 · cos((α + β) / 2) · cos((α − β) / 2)
```

When α ≈ β, the second factor `cos((α − β) / 2)` has very low frequency — it varies *slowly* across the image. That slow factor is the moiré envelope; the fast `cos((α + β) / 2)` rides on top of it. Where the slow factor is +1, the two source patterns add up to bright; where it's −1, they cancel to dark. The eye picks out the slow factor as a wide-band pattern even though neither source pattern alone shows it.

For two line patterns at angles 0° and 5° with the same wavelength λ = 6 px, the difference vector has magnitude `2 sin(2.5°/2) / λ ≈ 0.007` cycles per pixel — a wavelength of around 140 pixels for the moiré, vs. 6 pixels for the source. That's the visible band spacing.

### Concept 3 — Ring moiré

The same construction works with *radial* patterns. Two concentric-ring patterns with slightly displaced centres produce a different moiré geometry:

```python
cy1, cx1 = H/2 − 5, W/2 − 5
cy2, cx2 = H/2 + 5, W/2 + 5
r1 = np.sqrt((y − cy1)**2 + (x − cx1)**2)
r2 = np.sqrt((y − cy2)**2 + (x − cx2)**2)
rings = np.cos(r1 / 3) + np.cos(r2 / 3)
```

The moiré now forms curved bands radiating from somewhere between the two centres — the *hyperbolic* moiré geometry well-known to printers and printers of optical-illusion calendars [4].

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Technical note · moiré in security printing">
Bank notes, passports, and stock certificates often embed a fine moiré-prone pattern as an anti-counterfeiting measure. When photocopied or scanned, the source's sampling grid creates moiré that doesn't exist in the original, instantly visible to a trained operator. The trick relies on the photocopier's pixel grid being a regular pattern whose beating with the bank-note fine print becomes the visible moiré [1].
</Admonition>

## Exercises <a id="exercises"></a>

Three exercises in **Execute → Modify → Create** order: render line moiré, tune the angle, then build a ring moiré.

<Exercise n={1} kicker="EXECUTE" title="Render the line moiré">

Run `moire_lines.py` from the quick start.

**Reflection questions**

- The moiré bands appear *much wider* than the source lines. Why?
- What happens if `ANGLE_DEG = 0`?
- What if the two patterns had different *wavelengths* instead of different angles?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Wide bands** — the moiré frequency is the *difference* of the two source frequencies, which is small when the angle (or wavelength) difference is small. Small frequency → large wavelength → wide bands.

**Angle 0** — the two patterns become identical. Summing them is the same as one pattern at double amplitude. No moiré; just the source stripes.

**Different wavelengths** — same physics. If λ₁ = 6 and λ₂ = 7, the difference frequency creates a moiré whose wavelength is `λ₁ · λ₂ / |λ₂ − λ₁| = 42` pixels. The angle and wavelength differences are interchangeable knobs for moiré.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kicker="MODIFY" title="Three moiré variations">

Edit `moire_lines.py` for these goals.

**Goals**

1. **Wider bands** — drop `ANGLE_DEG` to `2`. Smaller angle → wider moiré bands.
2. **Crosshatch moiré** — bump `ANGLE_DEG` to `90`. The two patterns are perpendicular; no slow-beating moiré, just an interference grid.
3. **Wavelength moiré** — keep both angles at 0 but make the second pattern's wavelength 6.5 instead of 6. Beats from the wavelength mismatch.

Moiré wavelength roughly doubles. Bands become noticeably wider.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — what to expect">

```python

```

The patterns are *orthogonal* — they don't share enough frequency to beat. The result is a grid of bright dots where both cosines are positive — moiré is absent.

</Dropdown>

```python

WAVELENGTH_B = 6.5
```

Horizontal moiré bands at ~78-pixel wavelength. Same physics; different beating axis.

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="exercise3_solution.py">
```python
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

H, W = 400, 400
y, x = np.indices((H, W)).astype(np.float64)

cy1, cx1 = H/2 - 8, W/2 - 8
cy2, cx2 = H/2 + 8, W/2 + 8
r1 = np.sqrt((y - cy1)**2 + (x - cx1)**2)
r2 = np.sqrt((y - cy2)**2 + (x - cx2)**2)
rings = np.cos(r1 / 3) + np.cos(r2 / 3)
img = (rings - rings.min()) / (rings.max() - rings.min())
Image.fromarray((img * 255).astype(np.uint8), 'L').save('ring_moire.png')
```
</CodeBlock>

**How it works:**

- Each centre generates concentric rings via `cos(radius / wavelength)`.
- Summing the two ring patterns produces the hyperbolic moiré.
- The bands curve through the region between the two centres in the characteristic "hyperbolic" geometry.

</Dropdown>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Moiré patterns are the *visual* form of frequency beating. Sum two regular patterns at slightly different frequencies (or angles, which is the same thing) and the eye sees a low-frequency envelope — the difference of the two source frequencies. The same physics generates audio beats, optical illusions in textiles, and the security stripes on bank notes. In NumPy, build each source as a single cosine of a projected coordinate, and sum. The angle (or wavelength) difference controls the moiré wavelength: smaller difference, wider bands. The ring variant — two concentric-ring patterns with offset centres — gives a hyperbolic moiré geometry. The whole effect is two trig expressions added together.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Setting the angle difference to exactly 0 — the patterns become identical; no beating.
- Setting the angle difference to exactly 90° — the patterns become orthogonal; no shared frequency to beat.
- Forgetting to normalise the sum to `[0, 1]` — the cosine sum can reach -2 or +2 and `(z * 255).astype(uint8)` saturates.
- Using too large a wavelength — the pattern itself becomes visible only as a slow gradient; no moiré.
- Comparing to wave interference (6.4.2) — they share the cosine sum but moiré is about *visual* artifact frequency mismatch, interference is about *physical* wave superposition.
