## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

A noise sample is *coloured* by its power spectrum — how much energy lives at each frequency. **White** noise has a flat spectrum (equal power everywhere); **pink** noise has power that falls off as `1/f`; **brown** (or red) noise falls as `1/f²`. Each colour produces a recognisable texture, and you can generate any of them from the same FFT pipeline: white noise → multiply by `1/f^(β/2)` → inverse FFT. The construction is the spatial-domain counterpart of audio engineering's familiar colour terminology, and gives you a different *kind* of noise control than Perlin or Worley [1, 2].

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Define white, pink, brown, and blue noise by their power spectra (`1/f^β` with β = 0, 1, 2, −1).</li>
  <li>Generate coloured noise by reshaping white noise's FFT magnitude with a `1/f^(β/2)` mask.</li>
  <li>Recognise each colour by its texture — white = harsh, pink = balanced, brown = blobby, blue = peppered.</li>
  <li>Use coloured noise as a complement to Perlin/Worley: same coherent-noise question, different statistical knob.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — pink noise via FFT shaping <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def colored_noise(shape, beta, seed=0):
    """Power spectrum proportional to 1/f^beta."""
    h, w = shape
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    white = rng.standard_normal(shape)
    F = np.fft.fft2(white)

    fy = np.fft.fftfreq(h).reshape(-1, 1)
    fx = np.fft.fftfreq(w).reshape(1, -1)
    f = np.sqrt(fy * fy + fx * fx)
    f[0, 0] = 1.0                       # avoid divide by 0 at DC

    F = F / (f ** (beta / 2))           # reshape magnitude
    F[0, 0] = 0                         # drop the DC component

    z = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(F))
    return (z - z.min()) / (z.max() - z.min())

pink = colored_noise((256, 256), beta=1, seed=37)
Image.fromarray((pink * 255).astype(np.uint8), 'L').save('pink_noise.png')
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — Noise *colours* are spectral shapes

In audio engineering, **noise colours** describe how much energy lives at each frequency [3]. The naming convention transfers cleanly to 2D images:

| Colour | β   | Power spectrum | Visual feel |
|--------|-----|----------------|-------------|
| Blue   | −1  | `f`            | Peppered, fine grain |
| White  | 0   | `1` (constant) | TV static, harsh    |
| Pink   | 1   | `1/f`          | Balanced, natural   |
| Brown  | 2   | `1/f²`         | Smooth, blobby      |

The lower the exponent, the more energy at high frequencies, the harsher the texture. Pink noise is "natural" because many physical processes — heartbeats, river flows, music — show 1/f spectra [4].

### Concept 2 — Reshape spectra in the frequency domain

White noise has a flat power spectrum because it is i.i.d. Gaussian: every frequency carries the same expected energy. To turn white noise into a coloured variant, take the FFT, multiply by a frequency-dependent factor, and inverse-transform:

```python
F = np.fft.fft2(white)
F /= f ** (beta / 2)                # f = radial frequency
out = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(F))
```

Why `β/2` instead of `β`? Because power is the *square* of magnitude. To get a power spectrum that scales as `1/f^β` you scale the *magnitude* by `1/f^(β/2)`.

The radial frequency `f = sqrt(fy² + fx²)` is computed from `np.fft.fftfreq`, which returns the canonical frequency bin centres. The DC component (`f = 0`) needs special handling — divide by zero is bad — so the recipe sets it to 1 before the divide and zeros it after the multiply [5].

<Admonition type="important">
The output is not normalised by default — `np.real(np.fft.ifft2(...))` can have any range. Rescale to `[0, 1]` with min-max normalisation before converting to `uint8` for display.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — When each colour is useful

- **White noise** — dithering, randomised positions, decision-making. Bad for texture because the eye sees the harshness.
- **Pink noise** — natural-looking texture. Audio mastering uses pink noise as a reference because it sounds spectrally balanced. Visually, fine detail without fatigue.
- **Brown noise** — soft, blobby, almost-fractal. Useful as a base layer for terrain or volumetric clouds; close in spirit to Perlin fbm but with different exact statistics.
- **Blue noise** — fine speckle without low-frequency clumping. The current state-of-the-art dithering algorithm for high-quality image quantisation [6].

Picking a colour is picking *what kind* of randomness you want. Pink for natural-looking texture, blue for clean dithering, brown for terrain-like noise, white for raw randomness.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Technical note · why pink noise sounds natural">
1/f noise is the spectral signature of many systems exhibiting *self-similarity at multiple scales* — earthquakes, river floods, neuron firing patterns, even economic time series. There is no consensus explanation; "pink noise is everywhere" remains an unresolved theoretical question in statistical physics [4].
</Admonition>

<Exercise n={1} kicker="EXECUTE" title="Run the pink-noise generator">

Run `pink_noise.py` from the quick start. Compare it mentally to the random noise you saw in 6.1.1's reflection question.

**Reflection questions**

- Why is pink noise less "harsh" than white noise even though both are random?
- The script sets `F[0, 0] = 0` after the divide. Why?
- What does increasing `beta` to `3` produce?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Less harsh** — pink has *less* energy at high frequencies than white. The harshness of white noise comes from its high-frequency content (rapid pixel-to-pixel changes); damping those frequencies softens the texture.

**Zeroing DC** — the DC component (`F[0, 0]`) is the mean brightness of the image. Inverse-transforming a non-zero DC would produce a constant offset. Zeroing it ensures the output is mean-zero, which after normalisation gives full dynamic range.

**β = 3** — even steeper falloff than brown noise. The picture becomes *very* smooth — almost a soft gradient with subtle blobby variation. Useful as an under-layer; not useful as a texture on its own.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Complete solution">

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="exercise3_solution.py">
```python
import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def colored_noise(shape, beta, seed=0):
    h, w = shape
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    white = rng.standard_normal(shape)
    F = np.fft.fft2(white)
    fy = np.fft.fftfreq(h).reshape(-1, 1)
    fx = np.fft.fftfreq(w).reshape(1, -1)
    f = np.sqrt(fy * fy + fx * fx); f[0, 0] = 1
    F /= f ** (beta / 2); F[0, 0] = 0
    z = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(F))
    return (z - z.min()) / (z.max() - z.min())

brown = colored_noise((400, 400), beta=2, seed=11)
pink  = colored_noise((400, 400), beta=1, seed=22)
blend = 0.7 * brown + 0.3 * pink
blend = (blend - blend.min()) / (blend.max() - blend.min())

ramp = np.array([[0.15, 0.30, 0.10],
                 [0.50, 0.45, 0.20],
                 [0.95, 0.95, 0.95]])

t = blend
rgb = np.where(
    t[..., None] < 0.5,
    ramp[0] * (1 - 2*t)[..., None] + ramp[1] * (2*t)[..., None],
    ramp[1] * (1 - 2*(t-0.5))[..., None] + ramp[2] * (2*(t-0.5))[..., None],
)
rgb = (np.clip(rgb, 0, 1) * 255).astype(np.uint8)
Image.fromarray(rgb).save('terrain_blend.png')
```
</CodeBlock>

**How it works:**

- Brown noise gives the smooth base topology (the "shape" of the terrain).
- Pink noise gives the fine local variation (the "texture" of the terrain).
- Blending at 0.7:0.3 keeps the base structure dominant; the pink overlay adds character.
- A three-stop colour ramp maps `[0, 1]` to green → tan → white for a topographic look.

</Dropdown>

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Coloured noise lives in the frequency domain. Start from white (i.i.d. Gaussian), FFT it, multiply by `1/f^(β/2)`, inverse-FFT. Different β values produce different "colours" — flat (white), `1/f` (pink), `1/f²` (brown), `f` (blue) — each with a recognisable texture. Pink and brown are useful as natural-looking base layers; blue is the right choice for dithering and stochastic sampling because it has no low-frequency clumping. The same audio-engineering vocabulary transfers cleanly to 2D, and the implementation is one FFT + one element-wise multiply.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Forgetting the DC special case — `f[0, 0]` is zero, so the divide blows up. Set it to 1 before the divide, then zero `F[0, 0]` after.
- Using `f^β` instead of `f^(β/2)` — confuses magnitude with power.
- Not normalising the output — `ifft2` returns a complex array with arbitrary range; pull the real part and rescale before display.
- Comparing coloured noise to Perlin noise — different paradigm. Perlin shapes noise spatially (random grid + interpolation); coloured noise shapes it spectrally (FFT scaling).
- Calling pink noise "1/f noise" interchangeably with "1/f² noise" — `1/f` is pink, `1/f²` is brown. The exponent matters.
