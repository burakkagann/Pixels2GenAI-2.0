## Big Question — *what does it mean for a computer to "see" colours that belong together?* <a id="big-question"></a>

Every pixel of a photograph is a point in three-dimensional colour space — red, green, and blue running from 0 to 255 along three perpendicular axes. A picture of a sunset is, in those coordinates, a cloud of pixels concentrated around oranges, reds, and deep blues; a forest scene clusters around greens and browns. **K-Means clustering** is the cleanest algorithm for asking *"what are the N most representative colours in this cloud, and which pixel belongs to which one?"* Stuart Lloyd developed it in 1957 at Bell Labs to design pulse-code modulation quantisers; James MacQueen named it "k-means" in 1967 [1]. Seven decades later it is the workhorse of palette extraction, image compression, customer segmentation, anomaly detection, and most of "did Spotify guess my mood correctly" — anywhere a system needs to summarise a high-dimensional dataset by a small set of *centroids*.

The interesting part is not what the algorithm does — it is what it commits to. K-Means assumes (a) you already know K, the number of clusters, (b) clusters are roughly spherical in feature space, and (c) every point belongs to exactly one cluster. None of those assumptions are always true. Knowing *when* they fail and *how* to recognise the failure is what makes K-Means a building block rather than a hammer. Subsequent lessons in this subtopic (Mean-Shift in 7.1.2, DBSCAN in 7.1.3) systematically relax each of those assumptions.

## Part 1 — The image as a point cloud in RGB space <a id="part-1"></a>

A 384 × 256 colour image is 98,304 pixels — but they are not really pixels for our purposes. Reshape the array, drop the spatial structure, and what you have left is 98,304 *points* in three-dimensional colour space, each with an `(R, G, B)` coordinate between 0 and 255 on each axis. Each point is a vote: *"someone in this picture was this colour."* A photo of a desert sunset has nearly all its points concentrated in a swept arc through the orange-and-purple region of RGB space, with the rest of the cube nearly empty. A photo of a forest concentrates in the green-and-brown corner. **The shape of the cloud in RGB space is the colour content of the image.**

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="reshape_demo.py">
```python
# An RGB image of shape (H, W, 3) is just H*W points in 3D colour space.
pixels = image.reshape(-1, 3).astype(np.float32)
# pixels.shape == (98304, 3)
```
</CodeBlock>

Once you see the image this way, **colour quantisation becomes "find K representative points in the cloud, then snap every original point to its nearest representative."** The output image, when you reshape the snapped points back to `(H, W, 3)`, looks like a posterisation — the photograph is rendered using exactly K colours rather than the original 16.7 million. K=2 produces a black-and-white style image; K=6 produces something close to a hand-painted poster; K=64 is nearly indistinguishable from the original on most displays.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Lloyd's 1957 quantiser">
Stuart Lloyd's original paper was about *pulse-code modulation* — the analog-to-digital conversion that lets a telephone line carry voice. The problem: given a continuous voltage range, pick N discrete levels such that the average rounding error is minimised. He proved that the optimal levels satisfy a fixed-point condition — each level is the conditional mean of its quantisation interval — and that the iterative procedure (assign to nearest, then re-average) converges to that fixed point. The same algorithm now clusters colours in JPEG palettes, web-page colours in CSS, and pixels in every quantised-bit-depth game [1].
</Admonition>

## Part 2 — Lloyd's algorithm in two steps, forever <a id="part-2"></a>

Lloyd's algorithm is **two operations alternated**:

- **ASSIGN**: for every point, find the centre it is closest to. (Distances are Euclidean in RGB space.)
- **UPDATE**: for every centre, move it to the mean of all the points currently assigned to it.

Repeat until the assignment stops changing. Typically 5–20 iterations is enough; the algorithm is guaranteed to converge in finite time on any finite dataset [2].

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="lloyd_demo.py">
```python
def kmeans_from_scratch(pixels, k, num_iters, rng):
    # Init: pick k pixels uniformly at random
    init_idx = rng.choice(len(pixels), size=k, replace=False)
    centres = pixels[init_idx].copy()
    labels = np.zeros(len(pixels), dtype=int)

    for _ in range(num_iters):
        # ASSIGN: each pixel to nearest centre
        dist = np.linalg.norm(pixels[:, None] - centres[None, :], axis=-1)
        labels = dist.argmin(axis=1)

        # UPDATE: each centre to mean of its points
        for c in range(k):
            members = pixels[labels == c]
            if len(members) > 0:
                centres[c] = members.mean(axis=0)
    return centres, labels
```
</CodeBlock>

The mathematics underneath is small but pretty. Each iteration **strictly decreases** the total within-cluster sum of squares

`J = Σ_c Σ_{x ∈ C_c} ||x - centre_c||²`

unless the algorithm has converged, because both steps are coordinate-wise optimisations of `J`: the ASSIGN step minimises `J` over labels with centres fixed, and the UPDATE step minimises `J` over centres with labels fixed. The algorithm is a special case of Expectation-Maximisation [3].

<Admonition type="important">
The "alternating optimisation" pattern — fix one variable, optimise the other, swap — recurs throughout machine learning: EM for mixtures, alternating least squares for matrix factorisation, GAN training, even the inner loop of diffusion-model sampling. Once you see Lloyd's algorithm, you can recognise the pattern everywhere.
</Admonition>

## Part 3 — Where K-Means breaks <a id="part-3"></a>

K-Means is fast, simple, and deeply opinionated. It assumes three things about your data, and if any of them are wrong, the algorithm will *still* give you an answer — a wrong one. The art is recognising the failure modes.

**Failure 1 — wrong K.** K-Means cannot tell you how many clusters your data contains. You must pick K up front. Pick too low and you merge distinct populations into one fuzzy blob (one centroid covering both red and orange skies). Pick too high and you split coherent populations into arbitrary fragments. The **elbow method** plots `J(K)` for `K = 1, 2, 3, ...` and looks for the "knee" where the curve flattens — typically the right answer. The **silhouette score** measures how well each point fits its assigned cluster versus the next-best one [4].

**Failure 2 — non-spherical clusters.** K-Means partitions space by Voronoi cells around the centroids — straight-line boundaries. A long, thin diagonal cluster gets cut into pieces by perpendicular boundaries. A ring-shaped cluster (think the outer ring of a flower's pixel distribution) becomes nonsense. The fix is a different algorithm — Mean-Shift (lesson 7.1.2) tracks density modes, DBSCAN (7.1.3) follows density-connected paths.

**Failure 3 — outliers.** A centroid is the mean of its members, and means are non-robust. One outlying pixel pulls a centroid noticeably in its direction. If you have a cluster of 1000 pixels at `(50, 50, 50)` and one outlier at `(255, 255, 255)`, the centroid drifts to `(~50.2, ~50.2, ~50.2)` — still close, but the outlier was not "snapped to its nearest centre", it pulled the centre. K-Means++ initialisation [5] helps for the *start* but not the steady state; the cure is robust variants like *K-medoids* (use the actual closest point in the cluster, not the mean).

<Admonition type="note">
The Voronoi-cell intuition is the most important geometric mental picture for K-Means. Once you have the centroids, the assignment step *is* drawing the Voronoi diagram of the centroid set — each region is the set of points closer to that centroid than any other. If you can sketch the Voronoi diagram, you understand K-Means' output.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- Run `kmeans_palette.py` with K=2, K=6, and K=24. At what point does the quantised output become indistinguishable from the original to your eye?
- The reference fits on a 4000-pixel sub-sample, not all 98,304 pixels. Why is that almost equivalent? When would it fail?
- Two runs of K-Means on the same image with different random initialisations can produce **different** palettes. Why does the algorithm not always find the global optimum?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**K threshold for indistinguishability** — depends on the image, but typically K=16 to K=32 for natural photographs. The reason is that the human visual system can distinguish roughly 100 colours simultaneously in a single scene without effort; above K=64 the differences become subliminal. For synthetic images with smooth gradients, K=8 can already be hard to distinguish from the original.

**Sub-sample equivalence** — the centroids only depend on the *distribution* of pixels in RGB space, not on the exact positions. A uniformly-random 4% sample has the same distribution to within sampling error, and the centroids it produces are within 1-2% of the full-data centroids. This breaks if some colour is *rare but important* (a small bright red object on a dark background); the sub-sample might miss it entirely.

**Non-determinism** — `J` (the within-cluster sum of squares) is non-convex with respect to centroid positions. Different initialisations land in different local minima. The fix used in practice is `n_init=10` — run the algorithm ten times from different random starts and keep the best result. K-Means++ initialisation makes this much less of a problem by spacing the initial centres apart [5].

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Compare K-Means against Median-Cut quantisation** (the algorithm Pillow's `.quantize()` uses by default). On photographs, median-cut is faster but less accurate. On illustrations with flat regions, K-Means produces noticeably better colour boundaries.
- **Sort the palette by hue instead of luminance**. Convert centroids to HSV first, sort by `H`. The palette now reads like a rainbow strip rather than a brightness ramp.
- **Use K-Means in a non-image context**: cluster the pixel positions of edges in an edge-detected image to find "where the structure is". The same algorithm, different feature space.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

K-Means asks one question very efficiently: *what are the K most representative points in this cloud, and how do we partition the cloud around them?* The algorithm is Lloyd's two-step alternation: ASSIGN every point to its nearest centre, then UPDATE every centre to the mean of its points. The output is a Voronoi partition of feature space plus K centroids. Used on pixels in RGB space, it produces colour-quantised posterisations; used on customer data, it finds market segments; used on document embeddings, it finds topic clusters. The algorithm's three structural assumptions — known K, spherical clusters, mean-friendly distributions — are also its three known failure modes, and the rest of subtopic 7.1 (Mean-Shift, DBSCAN) systematically removes those assumptions one at a time.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Picking K by intuition. Always plot the elbow curve or compute silhouette scores at least once.
- Running K-Means once and trusting the result. Use `n_init=10` (sklearn default) or implement K-Means++ manually.
- Forgetting to scale features when clusters live in mixed units. RGB-only clustering does not need scaling (all axes are in 0–255); mixed-feature clustering does.
- Treating the labels as semantic. K-Means is unsupervised — the labels are arbitrary integers, not "cluster 1 = sky".
- Using K-Means on non-spherical clusters and reporting "the algorithm fails to find the structure." It is your *choice of algorithm* that fails, not the algorithm itself.
