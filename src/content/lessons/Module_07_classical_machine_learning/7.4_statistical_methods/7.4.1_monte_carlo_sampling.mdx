## Big Question — *can randomness solve problems that determinism cannot?* <a id="big-question"></a>

In 1946, Stanislaw Ulam was recovering from encephalitis and playing solitaire when he wondered: *what is the probability that a random game of solitaire actually solves?* He realised the combinatorial analysis was hopeless — but you could just *play many games and count*. He told John von Neumann, who realised the same trick would estimate neutron-diffusion integrals in the Los Alamos atom-bomb calculations, which had stumped analytical methods. They named the method after the Monte Carlo casino in Monaco where Ulam's uncle gambled [1]. **Monte Carlo methods** are the most powerful general-purpose computational technique for problems that have no closed-form solution. The recipe is one line: *replace any integral by an average of random samples.* Its error decreases as `1/√N` regardless of the integrand's dimensionality, which is uniquely powerful in high dimensions where every other numerical-integration method dies.

This is the first lesson in subtopic 7.4 ("Statistical methods"), which is unified by one idea: *probabilistic computation as a primitive*. Monte Carlo gives us the integral. Markov chains (next lesson) give us a way to *sample from any distribution we can evaluate*. Hidden Markov models (7.4.3) chain Markov chains together with observation noise. All three depend on Monte Carlo as the substrate.

## Part 1 — The strong law of large numbers <a id="part-1"></a>

The mathematical foundation of Monte Carlo is one of the oldest theorems in probability: *the Strong Law of Large Numbers*. For iid samples `X₁, X₂, ...` from a distribution `p`, with finite mean `μ`:

`(1/N) Σ Xᵢ → μ`  almost surely as `N → ∞`.

The integral `∫ f(x) p(x) dx` is just the expected value `E_p[f(X)]`. So if you can sample from `p`, the sample average `(1/N) Σ f(Xᵢ)` converges to the integral. That is the entire trick.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="pi_demo.py">
```python
def estimate_pi(num_samples, rng):
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=num_samples)
    y = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=num_samples)
    in_circle = (x * x + y * y) <= 1.0
    return 4.0 * in_circle.mean()
```
</CodeBlock>

Why does this estimate π? The probability that a uniform point in the 2×2 square lies inside the inscribed unit circle is `area_circle / area_square = π / 4`. Sample `N` points; the empirical fraction approaches `π / 4`; multiply by 4. The method works for *any* shape whose area you do not know how to compute analytically — irregular polygons, fractal boundaries, neural-network decision regions.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · ENIAC and the first MC simulation">
The first computer Monte Carlo simulation ran on ENIAC in 1948, computing neutron transport for early hydrogen-bomb design. Nicholas Metropolis (later of Metropolis-Hastings sampling) was on the team. The technique was classified for several years; the first public paper described an unclassified billiards problem [1].
</Admonition>

## Part 2 — The 1/√N convergence rate <a id="part-2"></a>

By the Central Limit Theorem, the Monte Carlo estimator `μ̂ = (1/N) Σ Xᵢ` is approximately normal with mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ / √N`, where `σ²` is the variance of one sample. The *root-mean-square error* therefore shrinks as `1/√N`.

This rate has a famous consequence: to halve the error, you need 4× the samples. To divide it by 10, you need 100×. Monte Carlo is *slow* compared to deterministic integration in 1D, where Simpson's rule converges as `1/N⁴`. But Simpson's rule's convergence rate degrades exponentially with dimension: in `d` dimensions it is roughly `1/N^(4/d)`. Past `d ≈ 4` Monte Carlo's dimension-independent rate becomes the *fastest* general-purpose method [2].

<Admonition type="important">
**Monte Carlo's headline property: error scales as `1/√N`, independent of dimension.** Every other classical numerical integration method (trapezoid, Simpson, Gaussian quadrature) suffers exponential degradation in high dimensions — the curse of dimensionality. Monte Carlo does not. This is why physics, finance, and ML use Monte Carlo (and its variance-reduced cousins) for high-D integrals.
</Admonition>

## Part 3 — When you cannot escape Monte Carlo <a id="part-3"></a>

Modern applications where Monte Carlo is the only feasible option:

- **Bayesian inference.** The posterior `p(θ | data) ∝ p(data | θ) p(θ)` is usually a 10-to-1000-dimensional integral. MCMC (Markov-chain Monte Carlo, Stan/PyMC/JAX) samples from it.
- **Reinforcement learning.** Value functions are expectations over future trajectories. MC rollouts (and Q-learning, which is MC plus bootstrapping) estimate them.
- **Pricing options** in quantitative finance. Path-dependent options need expected values over many stochastic paths.
- **Diffusion models.** The training objective and sampling procedures are both Monte Carlo over score functions.
- **Light transport** in computer graphics. The 6-dimensional `incoming light, outgoing light, surface position` integral has no analytical form; ray tracing is Monte Carlo over light paths [3].

The crucial caveat: vanilla Monte Carlo has *high variance* in high-dimensional integrals where the integrand is concentrated in a small region. Variance-reduction methods — importance sampling, control variates, stratified sampling — can reduce variance by orders of magnitude. Quasi-Monte Carlo (low-discrepancy sequences) achieves `1/N` convergence in moderate dimensions [4].

<Admonition type="note">
The first time you internalise "any integral can be a sample mean", the world of computational methods opens up. Renderers compute pixel colours by averaging random light paths. Reinforcement-learning agents estimate the value of a policy by averaging returns from random trajectories. Bayesian neural networks compute predictions by averaging over weights sampled from the posterior. The same primitive solves all of them.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- The π estimate after 100 samples is wildly inaccurate; after 10,000 it is much better; after 1,000,000 it is excellent. Quantify "much better" and "excellent" using the `1/√N` rule.
- Why is Monte Carlo *worse* than Simpson's rule for a smooth 1D integral, but *better* for a 6D integral?
- The 4D integral converges to about 1.05. With another 10× samples we could get more precision — but what would the same number of samples buy us if the function were 100-dimensional?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Quantifying convergence** — at `N = 100`, the expected error is `2/√100 = 0.2`, so π estimate uncertainty is roughly ±0.2 (true π ≈ 3.14, so we might land at 2.94 or 3.34). At `N = 10⁴`, error is ~0.02. At `N = 10⁶`, error is ~0.002. Each 100× increase in `N` halves the precision's order of magnitude — that is the price of the slow `1/√N` rate.

**Simpson's vs Monte Carlo by dimension** — Simpson's rule converges as `1/N^(4/d)`. In 1D, `1/N⁴` blows Monte Carlo's `1/√N` out of the water. In 6D, it is `1/N^(2/3)` versus `1/√N` — Monte Carlo wins, and the gap grows with dimension. Beyond ~4D, no classical quadrature scheme can compete.

**100D with same samples** — exactly the same. The `1/√N` rate is *dimension-free*. The same 10⁶ samples give the same error envelope on a 4D, 100D, or 100,000D integral (modulo the integrand's variance, which is a *different* concept and can grow with dimension). This dimension-freedom is Monte Carlo's superpower.

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Importance sampling**: instead of uniform samples, draw from a distribution `q` that is large where `f` is large. The variance can drop by orders of magnitude. The price: bias correction by `f(x) p(x) / q(x)`.
- **Sobol or Halton sequences**: instead of pseudo-random samples, use low-discrepancy quasi-random ones. The convergence rate becomes `(log N)^d / N` — close to `1/N` in moderate dimensions, with the same dimension-freedom.
- **Estimate the volume of a 4D unit hypersphere** by Monte Carlo. The closed form is `π²/2 ≈ 4.93`; check your estimator against it.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Monte Carlo methods replace any integral by an average of random samples. The Strong Law of Large Numbers guarantees convergence; the Central Limit Theorem gives an error rate of `1/√N`. This rate is slow in 1D but *dimension-independent*, which makes Monte Carlo the only feasible method for high-dimensional integrals. The pattern recurs across physics (statistical mechanics, neutron transport), finance (option pricing), ML (Bayesian inference, RL value estimation), and graphics (ray tracing). The whole subtopic 7.4 — Markov chains, hidden Markov models, MCMC — is layered on top of this primitive.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Using too few samples and reporting a confident estimate. Always report a confidence interval based on the empirical variance.
- Confusing the integrand's value range with the variance. Two integrands with the same expected value but different variance need vastly different sample counts.
- Reusing a pseudo-random seed for repeated estimates and then claiming the variance is low. Use a different seed for each run when measuring true Monte Carlo variance.
- Skipping variance reduction in production. Importance sampling alone can cut sample counts by 100× for well-chosen `q`.
- Forgetting that "iid" matters. If samples are correlated (e.g., from an unmixed MCMC chain), the `1/√N` rate fails.
