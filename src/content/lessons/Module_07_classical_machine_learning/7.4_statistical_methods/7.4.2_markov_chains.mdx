## Big Question — *can a memoryless model produce text that almost makes sense?* <a id="big-question"></a>

In 1906, Andrey Markov was arguing with the statistician Pavel Nekrasov about whether the law of large numbers required independence. To prove it did not, Markov took 20,000 letters of Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*, tabulated which letter followed which, and showed that the resulting *dependent* random walk still obeyed long-run statistical regularities [1]. He had inadvertently invented what we now call the **Markov chain**: a stochastic process where the next state depends only on the current state, not on the full history. The minimal data structure is a square *transition matrix* of probabilities. The applications, a century later, are astonishing: PageRank ranks the web by computing the stationary distribution of a Markov chain over hyperlinks. RNN language models are non-linear extensions of Markov chains. Diffusion image models are Markov chains over noise levels [2].

This lesson uses a character-level chain to keep the model interpretable and the data structure visible. Real-world Markov chains scale to millions of states (PageRank's webpages) or to continuous state spaces (statistical mechanics, finance), but the architecture is the same: transition matrix + sample step + (sometimes) stationary distribution.

## Part 1 — The Markov property <a id="part-1"></a>

A discrete-time stochastic process `X₀, X₁, X₂, ...` is a **Markov chain** if for every `t`:

`P(X_{t+1} | X_t, X_{t-1}, ..., X_0) = P(X_{t+1} | X_t)`

The future depends on the present, not the past. This is a strong assumption — most real-world sequences violate it (the next word in this sentence depends on much more than the previous word) — but it is also a powerful one. It lets you compress an arbitrarily long sequence into a single (`current state` × `next state`) lookup, which is exactly what a transition matrix is.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="transition_demo.py">
```python
def build_transition_matrix(text):
    chars = sorted(set(text))
    idx = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(chars)}
    n = len(chars)
    counts = np.zeros((n, n))
    for a, b in zip(text, text[1:]):
        counts[idx[a], idx[b]] += 1
    counts += 1.0                                  # Laplace smoothing
    return chars, counts / counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
```
</CodeBlock>

The Laplace smoothing (`counts += 1`) is critical: without it, any pair that does not appear in training data has probability zero, and the chain can get stuck if it ever lands in a state with no outgoing transitions. Adding 1 to every entry says "every pair is at least a little possible." This is the cheapest variant of *additive smoothing*; production systems use Kneser-Ney or other more sophisticated schemes [3].

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Markov's polemic">
Markov's 1906 paper was explicitly motivated by a *political* fight. Nekrasov, a religious mathematician, was arguing that statistical regularity required free will (random independent events). Markov, a militant atheist, wanted to demonstrate that even *dependent* sequences exhibit regularity. The Pushkin character-counting experiment was the proof. The Markov chain emerged as the by-product of a methodological debate that today would seem absurd, but it produced one of the most consequential data structures in modern computing [1].
</Admonition>

## Part 2 — Sampling and the output's character <a id="part-2"></a>

Once you have a transition matrix, sampling is trivial: pick a starting character, look up its row in the matrix, sample the next character from that row, repeat.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="sample_demo.py">
```python
def sample(transition, chars, length, seed_char, rng):
    idx = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(chars)}
    out = [seed_char]
    for _ in range(length - 1):
        row = transition[idx[out[-1]]]
        next_idx = rng.choice(len(chars), p=row)
        out.append(chars[next_idx])
    return ''.join(out)
```
</CodeBlock>

Sample 240 characters from our trained chain and you get text like:
```
thd .sthngvt u,n umea arlimyymmne sf mvwapghe.uel tmdu,. lof ie ofkcwabovhvinhywerggofnlvastw...
```

It is locally English-ish — `th` and `oo` and `re` are common, `qw` and `xb` are not — but it has no global structure. Words barely cohere. Sentences certainly do not. This is the **fundamental limit of first-order Markov text**: a single character of context is not enough to be linguistically coherent. The fixes ascend through history:

- **Higher-order chains**: state = last 5 characters, transition matrix is `(C^5) × C` shaped. Better locally, exponential explosion in state count.
- **Word-level chains**: state = last word. Captures more structure with fewer states.
- **n-gram language models**: maximum-likelihood transitions with elaborate smoothing. Dominated NLP from 1960 to 2010.
- **RNN and LSTM**: continuous hidden state, learned transitions. Dominated 2010 to 2017.
- **Transformers**: hidden state attends to entire history. Dominated 2017 to present. Still Markov *within* the attended context window.

All of these are descendants of the same recipe.

<Admonition type="important">
A higher-order Markov chain is still Markov — the **state** just includes more context. "First-order on character pairs" and "second-order on individual characters" are *the same model*. The distinction is conceptual, not mathematical. Generalising: "Markov" is not a property of the process; it is a property of how you define the state.
</Admonition>

## Part 3 — The stationary distribution and PageRank <a id="part-3"></a>

Run a Markov chain for a long time. Where do you end up? The answer is the **stationary distribution** — a probability vector `π` over states such that `π = π · T`. The state distribution at time `t` converges to `π` regardless of the starting state (under mild assumptions: irreducibility and aperiodicity).

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="stationary_demo.py">
```python
pi = np.full(n, 1.0 / n)
for _ in range(2000):
    pi = pi @ transition          # state distribution after one more step
# pi has converged to the stationary distribution
```
</CodeBlock>

For our character chain, `π` is the long-run character frequency — basically the empirical letter frequency in English, with spaces being the most common.

In 1998, Larry Page and Sergey Brin had the brilliant insight that the *importance* of a web page is the stationary probability of a random walker following hyperlinks ending up on it. **PageRank** is the stationary distribution of a Markov chain over the entire web graph [2]. Compute the chain's left eigenvector with eigenvalue 1, and you have ranked the internet. The same algorithm computes the rank-ability of nodes in social networks, citation graphs, and recommendation graphs.

<Admonition type="note">
A Markov chain is **ergodic** if it has a unique stationary distribution that any starting state converges to. The two conditions are *irreducibility* (any state can reach any other state) and *aperiodicity* (you can return to a state at irregular intervals). Most natural chains are ergodic; pathological ones (a chain on `{A, B}` with `A → B → A` deterministically) are not, and have multiple oscillating distributions.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- Read 240 characters of the sampled output. What does it get right? What does it get wrong?
- Higher-order Markov chains produce more coherent text. Why don't we just use a 50-character window?
- The space character has the largest stationary probability. Why is that not surprising?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Right vs wrong** — locally, character pairs are plausible: `th`, `oo`, `re`, `er`. Globally, words don't usually form ("thd .sthngvt" is not English) and grammar is absent. The chain captures character-level co-occurrence but knows nothing about word boundaries, syntax, or semantics. This is the *fundamental* limit of first-order Markov.

**50-char window** — exponential state explosion. With a 25-character vocabulary, a 50-character window has `25^50 ≈ 10^70` possible states. You cannot store, never mind train, a transition matrix that large. Higher-order chains are practical up to maybe length 5 with backoff smoothing; past that, you need a different model class (n-gram with sparse storage, or RNN with continuous state).

**Spaces dominate** — in English, spaces occur roughly once per 5 characters. The stationary distribution is essentially the empirical character frequency, and space is the most common "character." If you trained on text without spaces, `e` would dominate (most common English letter); with spaces in the alphabet, space wins.

</Dropdown>

#### Make it your own

- **Word-level chain**: train on `text.split()` instead of characters. Output is much more readable but smaller vocabulary diversity.
- **Bigram model**: state = pair of consecutive characters. Better local coherence; state count grows from 25 to 625.
- **Build PageRank**: treat the transition matrix as a graph, compute the stationary distribution, rank the "nodes" by their long-run probability. The pieces are identical to Google's 1998 algorithm.
- **Train on a different author**: Dickens vs Tolstoy. The transition matrices are visibly different; the stationary distributions slightly so.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

A Markov chain is a stochastic process where the next state depends only on the current state. Estimating the transition matrix from data is straightforward maximum likelihood with Laplace smoothing. Sampling is one-line. The stationary distribution — the chain's long-run behaviour — is the left eigenvector of the transition matrix with eigenvalue 1, computable by repeated matrix multiplication or directly via `np.linalg.eig`. The same architecture underpins PageRank, RNN language models, diffusion models, and most of modern computational physics. First-order character chains produce gibberish; the entire field of language modelling is about scaling the *state* without paying for the state-count explosion.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- Forgetting Laplace smoothing. Any character pair missing from training has probability zero and the chain gets stuck.
- Confusing "Markov chain" with "first-order Markov chain". The state can be anything you want; the property is conditional independence given the state.
- Computing the stationary distribution by simulation when matrix algebra is faster. Use `np.linalg.eig(T.T)` and find the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.
- Using a Markov chain on time series with long-range structure (stock prices, climate). The Markov assumption fails and predictions are dominated by short-term noise.
- Sampling from a transition matrix without normalising rows. The probabilities sum to less than 1 and `np.random.choice(p=...)` raises a strange error.
