## Overview <a id="overview"></a>

Magnetic field diagrams are a 19th-century invention — Michael Faraday's "lines of force" reasoning, formalised by Maxwell in the 1860s. The lines are not real; they are integral curves of the field, and the question of *how to draw them* is a question of numerical integration. In this lesson you will compute the 2D field of point magnetic dipoles, sum the contributions, and use `matplotlib.streamplot` to trace the streamlines. Animating one dipole's orientation produces a small but striking effect: at certain configurations the field lines *reconnect*, switching from "two separate loops" to "one combined loop" and back. Reconnection is the same phenomenon that powers solar flares and aurorae [1].

### Learning objectives

<ol class="obj-list">
  <li>Recognise that magnetic field lines are integral curves of a vector field — not physical objects.</li>
  <li>Apply the 2D point-dipole field formula and sum contributions from multiple dipoles.</li>
  <li>Use `matplotlib.streamplot` to trace streamlines through any 2D vector field.</li>
  <li>Watch field-line reconnection as a topological event when one source is rotated past a critical angle.</li>
</ol>

## Quick start — two dipoles, two streamlines <a id="quick-start"></a>

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="quick_start.py">
```python
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 160); y = np.linspace(-2.5, 2.5, 160)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
Bx_total, By_total = np.zeros_like(X), np.zeros_like(Y)
for pos, angle, strength in dipoles:
    Bx, By = dipole_field(X, Y, pos, angle, strength)
    Bx_total += Bx; By_total += By
ax.streamplot(X, Y, Bx_total, By_total, density=1.6)
```
</CodeBlock>

## Core concepts <a id="core-concepts"></a>

### Concept 1 — The 2D point-dipole formula

A magnetic dipole is a magnetic source with no monopole (no isolated "north" or "south"). Its field, written in vector form, is

```
B(r) = (3 (m · r̂) r̂ - m) / r³
```

where `m` is the dipole moment vector and `r` is the displacement from the dipole to the field point. In 2D code:

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="dipole_demo.py">
```python
def dipole_field(X, Y, position, angle, strength):
    dx, dy = X - position[0], Y - position[1]
    r2 = dx*dx + dy*dy + 0.02
    r  = np.sqrt(r2)
    mx, my = strength * np.cos(angle), strength * np.sin(angle)
    m_dot_r = mx*dx + my*dy
    Bx = (3 * m_dot_r * dx / r2 - mx) / (r2 * r)
    By = (3 * m_dot_r * dy / r2 - my) / (r2 * r)
    return Bx, By
```
</CodeBlock>

The `+ 0.02` in `r2` is a softening parameter — same idea as in lesson 5.3.2 N-Body. Without it, evaluating the field *at* a dipole's location divides by zero.

<Admonition type="dyk" title="Did you know? · Faraday's lines were a heuristic">
Faraday could not do calculus. He visualised lines of force as a way to *think about* electromagnetism without equations. Maxwell built his formal theory partly to give Faraday's mental pictures a mathematical foundation. The streamlines in this lesson are the modern continuation of that 1830s heuristic — still useful, still purely diagrammatic [1].
</Admonition>

### Concept 2 — Streamlines are integral curves

A streamline is the trajectory of a hypothetical "test particle" released into the field that always moves in the direction of the local field vector. Mathematically it is the solution of `dr/dt = B(r)` — an ordinary differential equation. `matplotlib.streamplot` uses a Runge-Kutta integrator internally to trace these curves on a sampled grid.

<CodeBlock lang="python" file="streamplot_demo.py">
```python
ax.streamplot(X, Y, Bx, By,
              density=1.6, linewidth=0.9,
              color=np.log1p(np.sqrt(Bx**2 + By**2)),
              cmap='cividis')
```
</CodeBlock>

Setting `color=` to the field magnitude colours each line by local strength, which highlights regions of focussed field (high colour values) versus regions of weak field (low values). Using `np.log1p` compresses the dynamic range so the colour map does not saturate at the dipole sources.

<Admonition type="important">
Streamlines never cross. If they appeared to, your sampled grid is too coarse — `streamplot` is doing its best with the data you gave it. Bump `GRID` from 160 to 250 if the rendering shows tangled crossings.
</Admonition>

### Concept 3 — Reconnection: a topological event

Two dipoles aligned head-to-tail (left north, right south) form two closed-loop bundles — one each side, connected end-to-end. Rotate the right dipole 180° (now both norths point inward) and the streamlines snap into a different topology: the loops now extend infinitely sideways. The transition is sharp — at some critical rotation angle, the two regimes are unstable and the streamlines reconfigure dramatically.

This is **magnetic reconnection** in its simplest manifestation. In the Sun's corona, the same mechanism stores energy in twisted field configurations and releases it as solar flares and coronal mass ejections [2].

<Admonition type="note">
The fact that a quantitatively-tiny change of one parameter produces a qualitatively-large change in the streamlines is the defining feature of a **topological transition**. The same kind of transition shows up in phase changes, in fluid-flow regime shifts, and in the Lyapunov exponent crossings of chaotic systems.
</Admonition>

**Reflection questions**

- Identify the frame where the streamlines visibly reconfigure. What angle is the right dipole at?
- Why does the centre of the canvas have very few streamlines at certain angles?
- What does `np.log1p` do to the colour scale, and why use it?

<Dropdown summary="Answers">

**Reconnection frame** — typically around frame 12 (90°) or frame 24 (180°), depending on starting orientation. At those angles the two dipole moments are perpendicular or anti-parallel, and the streamline topology changes.

**Few streamlines at the centre** — at the perpendicular configuration the field cancels along the perpendicular bisector of the line between the dipoles. The streamline tracer needs a nonzero field to follow, so the line-free zone is a *null line* of the field.

**`log1p` colour scale** — without it, the colour map is dominated by the very high field values near each dipole and the bulk of the canvas looks uniformly dim. `log1p` compresses the dynamic range, letting you see both the sources and the gentler bulk-field structure simultaneously.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

**Goals**

1. **Single dipole**: remove the second entry from `INITIAL_DIPOLES`. The classic dipole loop pattern from any physics textbook.
2. **Quadrupole**: four dipoles at corners of a square, alternating orientations. The field pattern is far richer than two dipoles.
3. **Same orientation**: both dipoles pointing the same direction. Streamlines merge into one big loop rather than two paired loops.
4. **Different strengths**: `strength = 1.0` vs `strength = 3.0`. The stronger dipole's field dominates near it; the weaker dipole is "absorbed".

<Dropdown summary="Goal 1 — what to expect">

The iconic single-dipole figure — two big loops above and below the dipole's centre, with the lines bending around through the centre. The starting page of every intro-physics chapter on magnetism.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 2 — what to expect">

A quadrupole. Field falls off faster with distance (`1/r⁵` instead of `1/r³`) and the pattern has rotational symmetry of order 2 — four lobes, two pointing in each diagonal direction.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 3 — what to expect">

The two dipoles read as a single elongated dipole at long range. The field lines wrap around both as if they were one bar magnet.

</Dropdown>

<Dropdown summary="Goal 4 — what to expect">

The stronger dipole's field zone is bigger; the weaker dipole's loops shrink and curl into the stronger one's. Asymmetric, more visually interesting than the equal-strength case.

</Dropdown>

</Exercise>

```python
def dipole_field(X, Y, position, angle, strength):
    dx = X - position[0]
    dy = Y - position[1]
    r2 = dx*dx + dy*dy + 0.02
    r = np.sqrt(r2)
    mx = strength * np.cos(angle)
    my = strength * np.sin(angle)
    m_dot_r = mx * dx + my * dy
```

`X` and `Y` are 2D arrays (from `meshgrid`); scalar operations broadcast across the whole grid. The whole function evaluates in a few milliseconds for a 160×160 grid.

## Summary <a id="summary"></a>

<Admonition title="Key takeaways">

Magnetic field lines are integral curves of a vector field; drawing them is numerical ODE integration, and `matplotlib.streamplot` does the heavy lifting. The 2D point-dipole formula is a one-line vector expression — sum contributions from multiple dipoles, hand the field to `streamplot`, and the visualisation falls out. The physically-interesting phenomenon is *reconnection*: a small change in dipole orientation can produce a large change in streamline topology, which is the mechanism behind solar flares and laboratory plasma instabilities.

</Admonition>

**Common pitfalls to avoid**

- No softening in `r²`. Evaluating the field *at* a dipole crashes the rasteriser.
- Using `streamplot` with too coarse a grid. Streamlines look tangled and crossing — increase `GRID`.
- Linear colour scale on the magnitude. The dipoles saturate; bulk structure invisible. Always `log1p` first.
- Forgetting `density=` parameter. The default of 1.0 is sparse for two-dipole fields; bump to ~1.6.
- Confusing "field line" with "force on a particle". Streamlines are static; they would not move a charged particle along their length the way they look.
